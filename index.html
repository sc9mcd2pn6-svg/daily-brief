US Midterms

Update: T−2 days — Economist says auguries good for Democrats; Trump rallies red-state danger zones

Two days before Tuesday’s midterms, The Economist publishes its final election forecast concluding that “the auguries are good for Democrats” — the party is heavily favoured to take back the House and remains competitive for Senate control. A Reuters/Ipsos poll puts Democrats nine points ahead on the generic ballot. President Trump spent the weekend holding emergency rallies in Kansas and Iowa, two states he won by double digits in 2024 but where Republican Senate incumbents are now trailing or tied.

The Economist’s electoral model points to minority voter shifts — particularly Hispanic and Asian voters moving sharply away from Republicans — as the decisive factor. A Democrat House majority would immediately reshape the legislative agenda and likely trigger congressional investigations into the Trump administration, affecting everything from the Russia sanctions waiver to the Justice Department’s election-interference lawsuits.

Source: Economist (Oct 31); Reuters/Ipsos; NYT Evening (Oct 30)

Economics

Trumponomics 2 years on: Economist delivers its verdict — ordinary Americans got something different

In its cover package ahead of the midterms, The Economist examines the economic record of Trump’s second term and concludes that while the president promised to help ordinary Americans, “he achieved something rather different.” Tariffs raised costs for lower and middle-income households; tax cuts delivered disproportionate benefits to the wealthy; and real wages for working Americans have struggled against persistent inflation that the Fed’s October hold (at 5%+) has failed to suppress.

The report notes that Trumponomics has created winners — asset-holders, defence contractors, domestic manufacturers shielded by tariffs — and losers, namely anyone dependent on imported goods, healthcare, or federal services. The finding matters for Tuesday’s vote: polling shows white voters without college degrees, Trump’s core base, have drifted away by double digits in several key states.

Source: Economist (Oct 31)

Health / US

Obamacare dealt another blow as 2027 premiums set to rise ~14% — second straight double-digit hike

Premiums for ACA marketplace plans will increase by an average of around 14% nationally for 2027 — on top of a 20% jump that hit enrollees this year — according to analysis by the healthcare research nonprofit KFF. The consecutive double-digit increases reflect a combination of rising medical costs, the Trump administration’s regulatory rollbacks that have weakened the ACA, and the expiration of pandemic-era enhanced subsidies that had temporarily kept premiums in check for millions of middle-income Americans.

The NYT notes the increases add to the mounting problems facing the programme that the Trump administration has steadily weakened. Middle-income people who do not qualify for the full subsidies are being squeezed hardest, and the issue is driving Democratic messaging in the final days of the midterm campaign.

Source: NYT Today’s Headlines (Oct 31); KFF analysis

UK / Commonwealth

UK prime minister signals openness to formal slavery apology — a major shift in British policy

Prime Minister Andy Burnham has said he is open to a formal British apology for slavery, according to the New York Times — a significant reversal from his predecessor Keir Starmer, who repeatedly declined to apologise and argued Britain should “look forward.” The comments come ahead of a CHOGM summit in Antigua where Caribbean nations have been pressing the UK for both a formal apology and a conversation on reparations. The shift sets up a pivotal diplomatic moment.

The UK has long resisted an apology on the grounds it would open the door to reparations payments. Burnham’s signal is unlikely to satisfy advocates who want concrete reparations, but it marks the first time a sitting British prime minister has publicly indicated a willingness to go there. The debate will deepen at the Commonwealth summit, which several Caribbean heads of government are expected to attend.

Source: NYT Today’s Headlines (Oct 31)

Update: Spain

Update: Spain train crash — traffic control errors confirmed; criminal investigation launched

Spain’s transport minister has confirmed that traffic control errors caused the high-speed train disaster near Urda on October 29 that killed 68 people, the country’s deadliest rail accident in over a decade. A controller authorised a train to proceed at high speed even though another train was already stalled on the same track. A judge has been assigned to investigate the controllers’ actions, and Spain’s three-day national mourning period has concluded. The official account marks a shift from initial uncertainties about the cause.

The crash has prompted urgent reviews of traffic control protocols on Spain’s high-speed rail network and has deepened political pressure on the Sánchez government, which faces questions about rail infrastructure investment and safety oversight.

Source: NYT Today’s Headlines (Oct 31)

Russia / Press Freedom

Kremlin shuts Russia’s last free newspaper — Novaya Gazeta placed on state enemies list

The Kremlin has forced the closure of Novaya Gazeta, placing it on Russia’s state enemies list, according to The Economist. The newspaper — co-founded by Mikhail Gorbachev and whose chief editor Dmitry Muratov shared the 2021 Nobel Peace Prize — had already been driven out of Russia in 2022 after the Ukraine invasion but continued operating in exile as Novaya Gazeta Europe. The final Russian domestic edition has now been shuttered, and the state-enemies designation threatens its exiled staff as well.

The move marks the end of an era for independent Russian journalism. The paper had for decades reported on official corruption, the Chechen wars, and crimes of the Putin state. Its silencing follows a string of similar closures that have effectively ended independent media inside Russia. NYT Evening (Oct 30) also noted the closure as a signal of tightening Kremlin control ahead of any negotiations over Ukraine.

Source: Economist (Oct 31); NYT Evening (Oct 30)

Africa / Politics

To understand African politics, listen to the music — artists capture a continent’s spirit of revolt

In a feature essay, The Economist argues that the best guide to African political sentiment is not polling or government communiqués but music. From Nigerian Afrobeats to Senegalese mbalax to South African amapiano, artists across the continent are channelling widespread frustration with authoritarian governance, corruption and economic exclusion. The essay traces how musicians have historically preceded protest movements — and how the current wave of music anticipates further political unrest.

The piece has direct relevance for anyone doing business across West Africa: the musical signal the Economist identifies reflects a genuine and growing popular impatience with incumbent governments in Nigeria, Ghana, Ivory Coast and Senegal, which creates both risk and opportunity for infrastructure and security businesses operating in those markets.

Source: Economist (Oct 31)

Update: France Strike

Update: France general strike T−4 days — Thursday Nov 5 action confirmed; SNCF disruption timetables due Monday

The November 5 nationwide strike called by all five major French union confederations (CGT, CFDT, FO, UNSA, FSU) is fully confirmed, with railway unions CGT-Cheminots, UNSA-Ferroviaire, SUD-Rail and CFDT-Cheminots all filing strike notices at SNCF. Rail service will be significantly disrupted on Thursday; SNCF is required to publish minimum service timetables at least 24 hours in advance, meaning disruption details will be clearest on Monday. TGV services out of Paris and intercity routes will be most affected. Metro and RER disruptions are also expected in Paris, though the RATP situation was still unclear as of Saturday.

The unions are protesting planned budget cuts, wage stagnation, and pension terms ahead of the 2027 budget vote. Italy and Belgium have seen parallel protests, and the action looks set to be the largest coordinated labour action in France in several years. Travellers should avoid Thursday or plan significant buffer time.

Source: SortiràParis; Connexion France; SNCF (Oct 31)

Lebanon Economy

Lebanese Eurobonds up 26% in 2026 — reform optimism, but IMF bank capital row unresolved

Lebanese sovereign Eurobonds have gained approximately 26% in value over 2026, with a sharp 12% rally in mid-October after the Economy Minister publicly said the Financial Gap Law — the centrepiece of Lebanon’s loss-allocation framework — would soon go to parliament. Bond markets took this as a signal of imminent IMF progress. However, analysts at Blominvest and international investors have flagged the rally as premature: no draft of the Financial Gap Law had reached cabinet as of late October, and a key sticking point with the IMF — the requirement for Lebanese banks to reach a 15% capital adequacy ratio within five to seven years, well above Basel III norms — remains unresolved.

Bank shareholders are reluctant to inject fresh capital without a signed IMF deal and clarity on Eurobond restructuring terms. The IMF and World Bank are expected to publish updated Lebanon economic projections later in November. BdL Governor’s year-end IMF breakthrough target remains on the table but far from certain.

Source: Blominvest; L’Orient Today; libnanews.com (Oct 2026)

Why it matters to you

An IMF deal — even a partial one — would be the clearest signal yet that Lebanon’s macroeconomic floor is holding. It directly affects client confidence, payment terms, and the business case for capital-intensive projects that Quasar bids for in Beirut. The 26% bond rally means money is coming back; but without the Financial Gap Law passing, dollar liquidity remains tight for the real economy.

Update: Lebanon Security

Update: Hezbollah disarmament — phase one near-complete south of Litani; second phase stalled

Lebanon’s army says the first phase of Hezbollah’s disarmament — removing heavy weapons from the area south of the Litani River — is nearly complete. However, Israeli authorities have publicly stated that progress remains “far from sufficient” and Israel continues to conduct strikes on what it calls Hezbollah infrastructure in southern Lebanon and the Bekaa. Hezbollah has refused to agree to any timeline for the second phase of disarmament, which covers the area between the Litani and Awali rivers, arguing the ceasefire deal does not extend to that zone.

Israel’s election result — with no bloc achieving a majority and Arab parties holding the kingmaker position — continues to cloud the diplomatic path forward, as any meaningful ceasefire consolidation requires a functional Israeli government with a clear mandate. The impasse keeps southern Lebanon fragile for the foreseeable future.

Source: Al-Monitor; Al Jazeera; L’Orient Today (late Oct/early Nov 2026)

Why it matters to you

The security stall in the south keeps Quasar’s reconstruction pipeline there on hold. Projects in Tyre, Nabatieh and the southern Bekaa cannot be properly scoped or priced until the zone is definitively clear of strike risk. Watch for any Israeli-government formation news next week — it is the key unlock for south Lebanon reconstruction demand.
