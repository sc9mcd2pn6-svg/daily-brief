💥 World · Gulf War

Houthis Kill 12, Wound 300 in Deadliest Saudi Airport Attack Since Iran War

Yemeni Houthi forces struck Riyadh’s King Khalid International Airport on Saturday with a barrage of ballistic missiles and drones, killing at least 12 people and wounding more than 300 — the deadliest single attack on Gulf Arab soil since the US-Israeli war with Iran began in February. A second strike hit Terminal 4 on Sunday, causing a major fire and forcing the airport to suspend operations for several hours. The New York Times called it a significant escalation from prior Houthi strikes, which had typically caused property damage and single-digit casualties.

The Economist this week devotes a major analysis to Saudi Arabia being “increasingly under siege,” arguing the kingdom has no good options: military retaliation risks Houthi counter-strikes on Aramco infrastructure, while a negotiated pause requires tacit US-Iran agreement that Washington is not currently offering. Brent crude pushed toward $105 Monday morning as traders priced in the threat to Gulf oil logistics. The Houthis continue to control the Bab-el-Mandeb strait, forcing shipping onto the longer Cape of Good Hope route.

✈️ World · Geopolitics

Trump Calls Ukraine to “Get a New President” — Kremlin Freezes Peace Talks

Hours after President Trump announced a bilateral deal allowing Russia to ship diesel to the United States, Kyiv launched a new series of drone strikes on Russian refineries — the kind of precision infrastructure attacks Washington has repeatedly asked Ukraine to stop. Trump responded furiously on Truth Social, saying Ukraine’s leadership should “get a new president” if it refuses to show restraint. The Kremlin announced within hours that there would be “no immediate resumption” of peace discussions, blaming Ukrainian actions for the impasse. The rupture effectively ends the diplomatic momentum that had been quietly building through Kushner and Witkoff’s Moscow visits.

The New York Times notes the disconnect is structural: Zelensky cannot stop attacking Russian energy infrastructure without giving up one of Ukraine’s few effective asymmetric advantages, while Trump cannot deliver a peace deal without Russian energy cooperation that Ukraine is actively undermining. NATO allies are alarmed by the personalisation of the crisis and the risk that Trump moves to a more hands-off posture ahead of November midterms.

IMF/World Bank Annual Meetings Open in Bangkok Today — A Decisive Week

The 2026 Annual Meetings of the IMF and World Bank open today (Oct 12) in Bangkok, running through October 18 with some 15,000 participants from 189 countries. The week’s calendar is unusually dense: the IMF’s World Economic Outlook drops Tuesday; Citigroup, Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan Chase and Wells Fargo kick off US major-bank earnings the same day; and US September CPI data lands Wednesday (August was 3.4% annual rate, above expectations). The IMF forecast last month called for ~3% global growth despite the Iran energy shock — markets will be watching for any downgrade.

The meetings carry direct significance for Lebanon: the IMF missions arriving in Beirut this week (see Lebanon section below) are formally briefed by the same teams presenting in Bangkok. The bond-market fragility theme — especially around France and other highly-leveraged European sovereigns — is expected to dominate side-sessions, with Lebanon’s Eurobond restructuring a case study in what happens when a sovereign delays reform too long.

📊 World · US Politics

US Midterms 3 Weeks Out: Economic Pain Puts Republicans on the Defensive

With November 3 midterm elections three weeks away, the New York Times reports that economic hardship — diesel at $6.23 a gallon, rising food prices, and the ongoing Iran-war energy shock — is expanding the electoral map into territory Republicans considered safe only months ago. A wave of Democratic polling leads (six-point generic ballot advantage in the latest Reuters/Ipsos) reflects public frustration with inflation, and Trump’s personal approval in the low-to-mid 40s offers little coattail effect. The House is currently 218-212 Republican with four vacancies.

The NYT notes that truckers, farmers and small manufacturers are among the most vocal dissenters: industries that voted solidly Republican in 2024 are now confronting margin compression from energy costs that the Trump administration has not been able to contain. Justice Department investigations into TV networks over Trump pool coverage have added to a sense of institutional stress that typically moves independent voters toward the opposition party. The GOP is banking on redistricting advantages in Florida, Texas and Missouri to limit losses.

🏴 World · Europe

Economist: Belarus Is Being Absorbed by Russia — Its National Identity Erased

A new Economist investigation documents how the Lukashenko regime, under mounting economic and political dependence on Moscow, is systematically dismantling Belarusian national culture: Belarusian-language instruction has been reduced to elective status in most schools, independent media operating from exile has been stripped of Belarusian domain access, and Russian state television now commands prime-time airtime across the country. The report finds Russian nationalism rather than Belarusian sovereignty has become the default identity framing in state institutions.

The Economist frames Belarus as a cautionary case for post-Soviet states: unlike Ukraine, which found its national identity galvanised by conflict, Belarus has had its political opposition shattered by the 2020 crackdown and lacks the civic infrastructure to resist creeping assimilation. Analysts quoted in the piece warn that within a decade the country risks becoming institutionally indistinguishable from a Russian federal subject.

📈 World · Society

Economist: Don’t Feel Sorry for Millennials — They’re Living It Up Now

This week’s Economist challenges the dominant narrative of millennial economic hardship with data showing the generation is quietly thriving across rich countries. Millennials (now in their 30s and early 40s) are out-earning their parents at the same age in most OECD countries, home-ownership rates are rising faster than in the 1990s as they hit peak earning years, and they are assuming senior management and political positions at record speed. The piece notes that the perception of struggle was largely anchored to the specific 2008–2020 period of low wages and unaffordable housing in coastal cities — a window that has since closed.

The analysis has implications for wealth transfer and consumption patterns: as Boomer estates are inherited over the next decade, millennial spending on art, real estate and luxury goods is projected to accelerate sharply. The Economist calls them “the comeback generation.”

🏭 Lebanon · Economy & Reform

Financial Gap Law: IMF Pressure Peaks This Week — Parliament Still Has No Vote Date

As IMF missions land in Beirut this week — coinciding with the Bangkok Annual Meetings — Lebanon’s parliament has still not scheduled a vote on the Financial Gap Law that the IMF has identified as the “last obstacle” to a staff-level deal worth an estimated $3 billion or more. According to L’Orient Today and a September 30 EnterpriseAM report, the fund will not back the current draft: it wants strict protection of the hierarchy of claims (depositors before shareholders before sovereign), no carve-outs for the Banque du Liban, and no transfer of old banking losses onto the state balance sheet — conditions Beirut’s draft currently fails on all three counts. The 2027 budget submitted October 2 was the first concrete test of whether the government would embed the VAT increase to 12% that the IMF also requires.

With Eurobonds holding at roughly 25 cents on the dollar, the market is still pricing in eventual IMF deal optionality. But BDL resistance and political hesitancy around loss allocation have created a stalemate that the reform window — before any electoral cycle complicates matters further — cannot absorb indefinitely. A failed or further-delayed Bangkok-week meeting between IMF staff and Lebanese officials would be a negative signal for creditors and diaspora investors alike.
