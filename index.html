🕵️ World · Geopolitics

CIA Director Ratcliffe makes unannounced secret trip to Moscow — US asked Ukraine to suspend strikes for visit

CIA Director John Ratcliffe flew aboard a US Air Force C-17 from Joint Base Andrews to Moscow on Tuesday — stopping in Riga, Latvia, on 23 August — in what CBS News, Bloomberg, the Washington Post and CNN all confirmed as an unannounced visit to Russian officials. It appears to be the first time a sitting CIA director has travelled to Russia in years. Neither Washington nor the Kremlin has formally commented; the Kremlin initially told journalists it had “no information” about the plane’s landing at Vnukovo Airport.

The most significant detail: the US reportedly told Ukraine that a senior American delegation would be travelling to Moscow and asked Kyiv to suspend drone and missile strikes while the delegation was in Russian airspace. Ukraine complied. The CIA has managed prior US-Russia channel including prisoner exchanges; but a personal visit by the Director at this scale goes well beyond logistics. Al Jazeera notes the Kremlin denied any planned talks with US envoys even as the visit was happening — suggesting the two sides are managing optics differently.

🌟 World · Culture

Dolly Parton dies at 80 — 25 No. 1 country singles, 200 million books donated to children

Dolly Parton, the Tennessee-born country-music icon, died Tuesday at the age of 80 after a brief battle with cancer, surrounded by family. Her nephew Brian Seaver announced the news. She held the top spot on country radio from 1967 to 1993, racked up 11 Grammy Awards and sold over 100 million records. Her philanthropic Imagination Library donated more than 200 million books to children worldwide. Andy Warhol called her “the glittery country goddess from Tennessee.”

The NYT’s evening edition led with her death and ran an extended obituary tracing her rise from a one-room Tennessee cabin to the Grand Ole Opry at 13 and five decades of cultural influence. NPR, NBC, CNN and virtually every major outlet led with the news Tuesday evening. She was, as the NYT’s fashion critic noted, a figure who “exploited rural stereotypes and kitsch to develop a chic all her own” while always maintaining her self-awareness: “I’ve created this and played it up, but it’s not even most of what I am.”

🍁 World · Trade War

Update: Canada retaliates with 50% tariffs on $20bn of US goods — Economist leads with “Carney must beware all-out trade war”

Canada formally announced retaliatory tariffs Tuesday, imposing levies of 15%, 25% and 50% across 700 American product categories covering $20 billion of US exports — including doubled duties on steel and aluminum, railway locomotives, smartphones and farm equipment. The measures take effect September 8. Ottawa simultaneously announced a C$7.5 billion business-support package including cash-flow facilities for SMEs and worker-support streams. Finance Minister Champagne said Canada would match US tariffs “dollar for dollar, rate for rate.”

The Economist made this its lead story Tuesday: “Mark Carney must beware an all-out trade war.” Its editorial argues Carney’s counter-tariffs are proportionate but that the situation could escalate dangerously — Canada sends 75% of its exports to the US. The Economist’s parallel piece asks whether Trump actually deserves credit for any manufacturing revival (its answer: very little). The NYT’s morning edition ran a detailed account of how the talks collapsed: “what Canada had to give up in exchange was seen as unthinkable.” Trump invoked a 1930 law that trade lawyers call obsolete; legal challenges are expected.

🦢 World · Public Health

Australia’s H5N1 bird flu spreads to all six states and first mammal — 236 wild-bird detections, poultry industry braced

Australia has confirmed H5 avian influenza in all six of its states, with the latest detection in a brown skua in north-western Tasmania and, critically, the first confirmed case in a mammal: a long-nosed fur seal at Beachport in South Australia. As of mid-August, authorities had recorded 236 confirmed H5 detections in wild birds. The agriculture ministry shifted to event-based reporting from August 12, indicating the scale of the outbreak has made individual-case tracking impractical.

Australia’s first H5N1 mass mortality event was confirmed in June in migratory seabirds; since then the virus has spread at pace that has strained government response, as described in Tuesday’s NYT morning edition. No poultry-sector outbreak has been confirmed, and human-health risk remains low — but the mammal detection is the key concern: H5N1 crossing into mammal hosts is the pathway that previous outbreaks (US mink farms, South American sea lions) have used to accumulate mutations. Australia’s poultry industry, one of the world’s most biosecure, is now on the highest alert in its history.

Economist: Africa’s “quiet tycoons” — secretive family conglomerates are the lifeblood of African capitalism

The Economist’s Tuesday edition ran its “Africa Inc.” feature on the secretive family business conglomerates that dominate African commerce from construction to banking to telecoms. Unlike the visible, listed companies most foreign investors track, these groups operate largely out of sight: privately held, multi-generational, diversified across sectors and geographies, and often the actual decision-makers when major contracts move. The Economist argues they are underestimated by outsiders and misread as cronyistic when many are genuinely efficient, capital-light operators.

For anyone doing business in Lagos, this framing matters. The data-centre boom in Nigeria — now a $374m market growing to $783m by 2031 — is attracting visible global names (Digital Realty, MTN, Equinix) but the land, construction, power and permitting decisions often flow through exactly these family conglomerate networks. Relationship capital with the right family groups can be worth more than any formal tender process.

🚨 Africa · Nigeria Security

Niger State orders emergency rescue of mass-kidnapped worshippers in Borgu — security forces deployed

Niger State authorities on Tuesday ordered all available security forces to rescue a large group of worshippers abducted in the Borgu local government area of north-western Nigeria, Premium Times reported. The Borgu district, bordering Benin, has been one of the most volatile corridors for armed bandit activity in Nigeria’s North-West, where hundreds of kidnapping incidents have occurred over the past two years. The scale and targeting of worshippers signals a continued pattern of organised criminality with ransom objectives.

The incident comes as Lagos remains calm but the broader Nigerian security context for expatriates and supply chains is a live risk. Premium Times noted Tuesday is also a federal public holiday (Eid ul Mawlid — Prophet’s birthday), meaning government offices are closed and official response capacity is reduced.

💥 Lebanon · Security

Israeli airstrikes kill 7 in Ansar, south Lebanon — Israel signals it wants “more serious” talks with Beirut

Israeli airstrikes struck the town of Ansar in south Lebanon and Deir Zahrani on Tuesday, killing at least seven people and wounding others, L’Orient Today reported in a live-blog update. The strikes came as Israeli officials sent a parallel diplomatic signal: Jerusalem has told US and European mediators it is prepared for “more serious” negotiations with Lebanon on a permanent security arrangement ahead of the December 31 UNIFIL withdrawal deadline. The dual military-diplomatic track — continued strikes alongside talk of negotiation — is Israel’s standard posture in the current phase.

The security situation in the south remains materially unresolved: UNIFIL recorded 137 projectiles per day and 1,700 airspace violations in a recent reporting period. With UNIFIL winding down on December 31, the December gap looms. Italy, France and Germany have each backed an EU-led successor force, but no formal UN or EU mandate for replacement exists yet. UPI (Aug 25) notes Lebanon is now racing to secure a UN-backed successor mission to fill what analysts increasingly call a “vacuum of peace.”

Why it matters to you

South Lebanon strikes directly affect client and project sites in the region. 📊 Lebanon · Economy & Reform

Lebanon Eurobonds up 19% year-to-date on FSDR momentum — IMF returns to Beirut in September as World Bank holds GDP at −6.4%

Lebanon’s BLOM Bond Index has risen 19.26% year-to-date, closing at 28.72 points with a weighted yield of 61.12%, driven by the FSDR banking reform law passed on 20 August and progress in the Lebanon-Israel Rome talks. The IMF, which called the FSDR a “major step,” is due to resume formal technical meetings in Beirut in September; those meetings will determine whether Lebanon qualifies for an Extended Fund Facility and what implementation timeline is required. A Constitutional Council challenge to the FSDR law remains a tail risk.

The macro backdrop remains harsh. The World Bank’s Lebanon Economic Monitor puts 2026 GDP at −6.4% (reversing last year’s 4.2% expansion) with inflation at 17.5%, driven by war-related supply disruption, displacement and falling tourism receipts. Beirut hotel occupancy remains below 25%. The creditor market is pricing in a restructuring deal within 12–18 months; the real economy is still contracting today. Both dynamics are simultaneously true and any projection must hold both.

BLOMINVEST weekly · Enterprise AM (24 Aug) · IMF statement (20 Aug) · World Bank Lebanon Economic Monitor (Aug 2026)
Why it matters to you

The Eurobond rally tells you sophisticated creditors are betting on a 2027 restructuring deal. That is the timeline for a banking-sector normalisation that would allow Lebanese SME clients to access credit again. Until then, cash-and-carry conditions remain: clients cannot borrow, so project finance depends on their own reserves or diaspora capital. Price your Lebanese contracts accordingly — collect advances, avoid extended credit terms.
