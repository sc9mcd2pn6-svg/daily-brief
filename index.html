Trump Claims 55% US Stake in Venezuela’s 65-Billion-Barrel Oil Reserves

President Trump announced on Friday night what he called “THE BIGGEST OIL DEAL IN WORLD HISTORY”: an agreement giving the United States a 55% effective output stake in a new private company that will develop 17 Venezuelan oil fields holding a proven potential of 65 billion barrels of reserves. The deal was negotiated by Rubio, Hegseth, and Venezuelan counterpart Delcy Rodríguez following Maduro’s capture and extradition in January. Under the terms, the US (via a private-sector partner, likely Chevron) also secures rights to purchase oil at cost, and Venezuela would use revenue exclusively to purchase American-made goods — a kind of oil-for-exports barter.

The NYT, leading with this story on Saturday morning, notes deep scepticism from energy analysts. The deal could theoretically attract $100 billion in investment and yield $209 billion in taxes for Caracas, but Venezuela’s infrastructure has decayed severely under two decades of mismanagement, and similar deals under the Maduro era were repeatedly promised and never executed at scale. US oil majors will want legal clarity before committing capital. Still, even a partial materialisation would significantly change the Western Hemisphere’s oil supply picture and could bring Brent down from its current $92 over the next two years.

NYT Today’s Headlines (anchor, 29 Aug) · CNBC · Washington Post · ABC News
Why it matters to you

If this deal begins to deliver Venezuelan barrels to market, Brent crude could soften meaningfully from its current Iran-driven highs. Lower oil prices reduce shipping and generator fuel costs for your Lagos and Beirut operations — and could relieve some pressure on Lebanon’s import bill. But the deal is almost certainly 2–3 years from affecting physical supply, so near-term Brent and diesel costs remain elevated.

🚢 World · Iran War

Update: UAE Tankers Attacked in Hormuz; Iran Charges $2M Toll per Vessel; Qatar Reports Progress

The UAE confirmed on Saturday that two vessels linked to Abu Dhabi National Oil Company were attacked in the Strait of Hormuz, with Iran accused of being behind the strikes — the most direct escalation involving a Gulf state since the Hormuz naval blockade began in May. The IRGC’s so-called “Persian Gulf Strait Authority” is now documented as charging per-vessel tolls of up to $2 million, payable in Chinese yuan, Bitcoin, or USDT. Western-allied commercial shipping has been effectively absent from the strait since early May. US Strategic Petroleum Reserve stockpiles remain near their 1982 low.

Simultaneously, Qatari mediators confirmed that “progress has been made” in US-Iran negotiations, with Iran reported to be preparing a list of conditions for reopening the strait, including ending the regional war. The NYT frames the overall conflict as Trump’s own “forever war”: no clear goals, no end in sight, consuming political capital without strategic payoff. Iran’s leadership appears likely to survive, and ordinary Iranians described in the paper as “hungry, confused, and unable to find a way out.”

NYT Today’s Headlines (anchor, 29 Aug) · Al Jazeera · Britannica/GlobalSecurity · Guardian (Wikipedia current events)
Why it matters to you

The UAE tanker attacks represent a direct escalation toward Gulf state assets — raising war-risk insurance rates and supply chain costs for any clients whose goods transit the Gulf. For Quasar Nigeria, logistics from Asia or Europe through the Middle East remain more expensive and less predictable. Watch for spillover into Lebanon if the UAE attacks provoke a US response.

🗳️ World · Geopolitics

Update: Iceland EU Referendum Too Close to Call — Counting Underway After Polls Close

Voting in Iceland’s historic consultative referendum on restarting EU accession talks closed on Saturday evening with the outcome entirely uncertain. Al Jazeera reported that early results showed the “yes” camp narrowly leading hours after polls closed, but the race was too close to call. The last pre-vote Gallup poll had the “no” camp at 51.6% and “yes” at 48.4% — within the statistical margin of error. A yes would instruct the government to open accession negotiations with Brussels; accession itself would take at least a decade and face further referenda.

The result, expected to become clearer later Sunday, matters beyond Iceland: it would be the EU’s most significant enlargement signal since Brexit and could galvanise similar debates in Switzerland and Norway. Key drivers of the yes camp were Trump’s Arctic territorial posturing and the security lesson of Ukraine; the no camp emphasised fishing rights and cultural sovereignty. Brussels is watching closely.

🦍 World · US Politics

Economist: Trump’s Faltering Presidency Makes Him More Dangerous, Not Less

The Economist’s Saturday “Telegram” column offers a sharp pre-midterm warning: as Trump’s presidency visibly flounders — the Iran war stuck, trade wars damaging the US economy, Republican losses in November expected by most forecasters — he should not be expected to behave like a conventional “lame duck.” Instead, the columnist predicts Trump will fight back “like an ageing silverback gorilla defending his dominance,” using executive orders, pardons, and federal agency power far more aggressively in a post-midterm period when he has nothing left to lose politically.

The piece warns that the period from November 2026 through January 2027 — when a newly elected Congress is seated — could be the most volatile of Trump’s second term. An American president with weakened Congressional backing but full executive power and a personal grievance against the system is historically the most unpredictable configuration in US governance. Sanctions use, tariff escalation, and judicial appointments are the likely instruments.

💉 World · Science

Economist: “It Now Seems Possible to Vaccinate Against Cancer”

The Economist’s “Well Informed” section makes its most bullish statement yet on cancer immunisation: a synthesis of recent trial data — including the Merck/Moderna mRNA melanoma vaccine and several new solid-tumour programmes — leads the editors to conclude that cancer vaccination has crossed from theoretical aspiration to plausible reality, at least for a subset of cancers. The latest results they review are “promising but not yet definitive,” a careful hedge, but the framing signals a paradigm shift in how oncologists and biotech investors are viewing immunotherapy.

The critical caveat is personalisation cost: mRNA cancer vaccines must currently be manufactured individually for each patient based on tumour sequencing, making them expensive and logistically complex to deploy at scale. The Economist notes that costs will need to fall by 80–90% before this becomes a standard-of-care treatment, which is realistically a 7–10 year timeline. Still, for early adopters with means, this is a rapidly closing window on trial access.

Middle East Crisis Exposes Nigeria’s 90% Offshore Cloud Dependency as Dangerous Vulnerability

An investigation by Guardian Nigeria frames the Middle East conflict and its disruption to regional data infrastructure as a wake-up call for Nigerian businesses: approximately 90% of Nigeria’s enterprise data is stored on cloud servers outside the country, primarily in Europe, the US, and South African hubs. The Hormuz closure and the threat of US-Iran conflict spilling into Gulf digital infrastructure has exposed how a regional crisis can cascade into business continuity failures thousands of miles away. BusinessDay reports Nigeria is eyeing a $1.9 billion data centre boom as a result, with the CBN’s January 2027 onshore data mandate providing regulatory urgency.

The Lagos-Lekki corridor now hosts 21 operational data centres with five more under construction, but the build-out is outpacing local power reliability — grid instability remains the single biggest threat to the data centre race across the continent, according to This Day Live. The Middle East digital vulnerability angle has given new political momentum to the CBN mandate and to government procurement of sovereign cloud infrastructure, opening a larger market for physical security integrators as new facilities come online.

🌊 France · Environment

France at Peak Drought: 92 Departments Under Water Restrictions, 62 at Crisis Level

France is experiencing one of its most severe August droughts on record: as of August 21, 92 of the country’s 101 departments on mainland France and Corsica are enforcing mandatory water restrictions, and 62 have declared the highest “crisis” level, banning pool filling, lawn watering, car washing and limiting industrial water use. The restrictions come on top of an exceptionally hot summer — the Paris region recorded eight separate days above 39°C in July and August — and follow a winter with precipitation 30% below historic averages across the south and southwest.

The agricultural sector is facing significant losses, with the wine harvest in Bordeaux and the Rhône valley forecast at 25–40% below a normal year. French authorities are debating fast-tracking desalination approvals for coastal municipalities. The water crisis adds pressure to the government of PM Lucie Castets ahead of what is expected to be a difficult autumn budget debate, with the RN and the left both positioning to attack the government’s infrastructure spending record.

🇱🇧 Lebanon
🪖 Lebanon · Security

Katz: IDF Will Not Leave South Lebanon Until Hezbollah Is Fully Disarmed — Lebanese PM Condemns “Systematic Destruction”

Israeli Defence Minister Israel Katz stated in an interview published Saturday that the IDF will maintain its presence in southern Lebanon until Hezbollah is completely disarmed — an explicit condition that Hezbollah has refused and that no previous round of negotiations has produced. L’Orient Today reports that Lebanese PM Nawaf Salam responded by condemning what he called Israel’s “systematic destruction” of villages in the south, calling it a clear violation of international law. UNIFIL separately documented more than 1,800 projectiles fired by Israeli forces in southern Lebanon in the seven weeks between June 21 and August 11, the first phase of the June ceasefire framework.

On a more hopeful note, UNIFIL formally transferred its first military position in the Khardali-Litani area to the Lebanese army’s 7th Brigade on August 20 — the first concrete handover in the process of transitioning UNIFIL’s 48-year presence to Lebanese state authority before the December 31 mandate expiry. However, the pace of handover is far behind schedule, and Israel’s refusal to withdraw from southern territory it occupies creates an unresolved overlap between IDF positions and Lebanese army authority. L’Orient Today also flags that the US-Iran memorandum of understanding being negotiated includes a clause on ending the Lebanon ceasefire arrangements — meaning Lebanon’s southern security is directly tied to the Hormuz negotiations.

L’Orient Today (Tier 3 independent) · Times of Israel · Arab News · Al Jazeera · UNIFIL
Why it matters to you

Katz’s unconditional posture effectively signals a prolonged Israeli military presence in the south, maintaining security uncertainty that depresses investment and client confidence in that region. For Quasar Lebanon, projects in the south remain high-risk pipeline; focus near-term revenue on Beirut and the north. The link between Lebanon’s security and the US-Iran Hormuz talks also means any breakthrough — or breakdown — in those talks could rapidly change the security calculus here.
