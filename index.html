Canada-US trade war: talks collapse, 50% tariffs live — Carney retaliates September 8

After last-ditch negotiations failed Friday night, the Trump administration imposed 50% tariffs on $20 billion in Canadian goods — covering dairy, electronics, furniture, lumber, plastics and clothing — effective immediately. Prime Minister Carney called the move “a miscalculation,” suspended talks, and announced Canada will match Washington “dollar for dollar” with retaliatory tariffs on US steel, dairy and electronics starting September 8. The NYT notes this is the deepest rupture between the two historic allies since the trade relationship was rebuilt in the 1990s.

The White House frames its tariffs as a response to Canada’s “discriminatory treatment” of American dairy and auto exports; Ottawa says the Trump demands are “maximalist and unacceptable.” RBC Economics warns the standoff will shave at least 0.8 percentage points off Canadian GDP growth and raise consumer prices in both countries through year-end.

Update: Brent hits $94 as Iran-Oman Hormuz talks stall — Economist warns on freedom of navigation

Brent crude rose to $94.24, its highest since July, as diplomacy between Iran and Oman over Hormuz passage rights failed to produce any agreement. Iran has maintained its “Persian Gulf Strait Authority” policy, requiring vessels to obtain passage permits, keeping effective closure in place. Treasury Secretary Bessent is pursuing “the greatest coordinated economic isolation in history” against Tehran, but Tehran publicly dismissed this as a distraction from America’s $40 trillion debt.

The Economist’s Free Exchange column this week ("The high cost of phantom tollbooths") argues that freedom of navigation is worth preserving as a global economic principle — every week of Hormuz disruption adds cost to every supply chain that touches the Gulf. Gold hit $4,662/oz as investors hedge the energy-inflation scenario.

🏭 Africa · Energy

Dangote offers Kenya, Ethiopia and Rwanda 30% equity in planned $17bn East Africa refinery

At a capital markets forum in Nairobi on August 21, Dangote Group offered East African nations a combined 30% stake in a massive planned refinery at Lamu, Kenya — with a capacity of 700,000 barrels per day, roughly double Nigeria’s Dangote Lagos refinery. Kenya is slated for 10%, Ethiopia and Rwanda are negotiating their shares. Construction is expected to start by end-2026 with a four-year build timeline. Bloomberg broke the story; Premium Times confirmed it from Nairobi.

The deal would be Africa’s largest single energy infrastructure investment and is designed to end East Africa’s dependence on imported refined products. For West Africa, it signals that Dangote’s ambition is continental — a rival refining hub could eventually affect fuel pricing dynamics across the region.

The operators who solve power and water will capture the market; those who don’t will face client churn as uptime SLAs bite.

🏦 Lebanon · Economy

IMF to resume Beirut meetings in September — FSDR implementation now the critical test

Following parliament’s passage of the Financial Sector Development and Recovery (FSDR) law last week, the IMF confirmed it will resume in-country meetings in Beirut next month. The Fund welcomed the legislation as “a major step” but issued a pointed warning: “effective implementation of this new bank resolution framework is critical.” Specifically, the IMF is monitoring whether the draft law will be applied in a manner consistent with international principles — past Lebanese reforms have been diluted in implementation.

The path to an IMF Extended Fund Facility (EFF) now hinges on the Central Bank and judiciary executing the law without political interference. Depositor recovery, bank recapitalization, and the formal restructuring of Eurobond debt (yield still ~61%, BBI 28.72) cannot advance until the framework is proven to work. The IMF’s September visit will be its first credible checkpoint.

Reuters · New Arab · BLOMINVEST · Investing.com
Why it matters to you

A successful IMF review in September would be the first genuine signal that Lebanon’s banking reform is on track. That could begin to unlock diaspora and foreign capital — the only realistic buyers of Beirut real estate right now. TikTok settles US child-privacy lawsuit for $400 million — largest-ever children’s data penalty

The US Department of Justice resolved a multi-year lawsuit accusing TikTok of illegally collecting personal data from children, reaching a $400 million settlement — the largest in US history for a children’s privacy case. The case centred on TikTok’s failure to obtain verifiable parental consent before collecting data from users under 13, and its continued targeting of minors with personalised content after earlier FTC settlements. The NYT reports the agreement includes mandatory audits and enhanced parental-consent mechanisms.

The settlement signals that US regulators are applying maximum financial pressure on Chinese-owned platforms regardless of TikTok’s US ownership restructuring negotiations. It also accelerates the broader global regulatory trend toward child-data protection — EU and UK legislators are watching closely.
