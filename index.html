International Law

ICJ finds Myanmar guilty of genocide — first state ever held to account under the Genocide Convention

The International Court of Justice ruled on 19 November that Myanmar committed genocide against Rohingya Muslims, in a 9–6 decision brought by The Gambia. ICJ President Yuji Iwasawa said “the only reasonable inference that can explain Myanmar’s actions is an intention to destroy” the Rohingya population in northern Rakhine State. It is the first time in the court’s 80-year history that any state has been found guilty under the 1948 Genocide Convention.

The ruling covers the 2017 military campaign in which at least 10,000 Rohingya were killed, hundreds of villages torched and over 750,000 people fled in what was described as the most rapid exodus in recent history. Myanmar is ordered to investigate, prosecute and punish perpetrators and pass laws to implement the Convention; reparations are to be determined later. The ICJ has no independent enforcement mechanism and Myanmar’s compliance remains uncertain.

At Cox’s Bazar in Bangladesh — home to 1.2 million Rohingya refugees — crowds cheered when the ruling was announced. NYT correspondent Hannah Beech reported scenes of jubilation and weeping at the camps.

Sources: NYT Evening (19 Nov); Al Jazeera (19 Nov); CNN (19 Nov)

Economist cover: China, Russia, Iran and North Korea — an “axis of malice” reshaping global order

This week’s Economist lead story describes a deepening collaboration among four authoritarian states — China, Russia, Iran and North Korea — that it calls “the axis of malice.” The coordination has intensified sharply since Russia’s full-scale Ukraine invasion: Russian drones killing Ukrainians depend on Iranian designs and Chinese components; North Korea has sent soldiers and factory workers to Russia; Chinese payment systems help axis members dodge sanctions; and their UN delegates vote in lockstep. Deputy editor Robert Guest writes that while this is not a formal military alliance, all four regimes share a core interest in “making the world safe for autocrats.”

The Economist argues the more consequential story behind the Trump–Xi–Putin Shenzhen summit is not the meeting itself but the structural deepening of this bloc. If China ever invades Taiwan, says the analysis, Russia, Iran or North Korea are likely to simultaneously provoke a crisis elsewhere to divide Western attention. The editors call for the West to treat this coordination as a systemic threat rather than a series of bilateral problems.

Sources: The Economist (19 Nov — cover, “Four colluding powers that threaten the West”)

Nigeria / Data Centres

Nigeria’s CBN orders all payment data stored locally by January 2027 — forcing fintechs off foreign clouds and into Lagos data centres

The Central Bank of Nigeria has mandated that all payment transaction data generated in Nigeria be stored and processed on local servers by 1 January 2027, with no extension granted. The rule covers banks, fintechs, mobile money operators and all payment service providers. It also requires companies to maintain current records of ultimate beneficial owners and provide them on demand. CBN confirmed the deadline is firm after industry lobbying for more time.

Industry analysts estimate that over 90% of Nigerian fintech firms and digital banks currently store workloads on foreign cloud infrastructure — primarily AWS and Google Cloud. Most Tier-1 and Tier-2 commercial banks have already localized, making mid-size fintechs the primary affected segment. BusinessDay.ng reported that migration concerns centre on cost, risk and whether local co-location can reliably carry payment workloads. Operators note that domestic data-centre capacity in Lagos has expanded significantly and can absorb the load, but the 42-day deadline is tight.

Sources: BusinessDay.ng; Arise.tv; Guardian.ng (Nigerian); Techpoint Africa; Tech.Africa

Energy / Economy

Qatar–Lebanon power deal advancing: 1,650 MW in new plants and a 20-year LNG supply agreement close to finalisation

Prime Minister Nawaf Salam returned from Doha on 15 November reporting “significant progress” on a landmark energy deal that would see Qatar finance, build and operate two 825 MW combined-cycle gas power plants at Deir Ammar and Zahrani, plus a 20-year LNG supply agreement with QatarEnergy. Under the current draft, Doha bears all upfront capital costs and Lebanon makes no initial Treasury outlay. The National reported Beirut and Doha are in “final stages” of negotiating guarantees and payment obligations; L’Orient Today confirmed Qatar has pledged to operate the project independently of EDL.

Lebanon’s state utility Electricité du Liban currently supplies only a few hours of power per day, with generators filling the gap. The proposed deal — which would more than double Lebanon’s generation capacity — would run gas via a pipeline through Syria, an arrangement Salam described as commercially advanced. EDL has this month also begun cutting power to public institutions that have failed to pay their bills, adding urgency to the broader reform agenda.

Sources: The National (15 Nov); L’Orient Today (Nov 2026); Al Jazeera
