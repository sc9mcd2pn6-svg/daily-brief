🧮 World · AI Breakthrough

OpenAI AI Solves Navier-Stokes “Millennium Problem” in 88 Hours

OpenAI announced on Tuesday that an unreleased next-generation AI model — running 10,000 concurrent agents — produced a formally verified proof of the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of mathematics’ seven Millennium Prize Problems carrying a $1 million award each. The problem, unsolved for roughly 90 years, asks whether smooth three-dimensional fluid motion can break down under the equations governing liquid and gas flow. The AI found that it can, identifying a “finite-time blowup” configuration. The proof is written in Lean, a formal theorem-verification language, and has been submitted to mathematicians for independent review. Nature and Quanta Magazine both confirmed the result is serious and contested in scope, not a trivial claim.

This is qualitatively different from earlier AI math achievements: a fully formal proof of a conjecture that has stumped human mathematicians across generations. The implications extend from weather prediction to turbine design and fluid-based engineering. OpenAI’s model used remains unreleased.

Update: Houthis Strike Saudi Aramco Sites — Oil Toward $100, 73 Wounded

Iran-aligned Houthi militants launched a large-scale drone and ballistic missile attack against Saudi Aramco energy installations and other infrastructure across southern Saudi Arabia on Tuesday, injuring at least 73 people. Saudi Arabia paused operations at several facilities near the Yemen border. Brent crude surged to $98.61 per barrel, its highest since the early months of the conflict, as traders priced in supply risk from the Arabian Peninsula directly. This is a new escalation beyond the established Hormuz tanker war — now Houthis are targeting the source of Saudi production, not just transit routes.

Goldman Sachs raised its December 2026 Brent forecast by $5 and now prices $100 oil as a realistic near-term outcome if further Saudi infrastructure is damaged. Iran separately fired ballistic missiles at US warships near Jordan overnight; all were intercepted or fell harmlessly, but the pattern of simultaneous pressure points is unmistakable.

UK, Canada and France Ban Trade With Israeli West Bank Settlements, Citing “Ethnic Cleansing”

Britain, Canada and France announced coordinated new sanctions Tuesday accusing Israeli settlers in the occupied West Bank of committing “ethnic cleansing” and banning trade in goods from settlements. The moves, by three nations that were until recently firm Israeli allies, mark a dramatic escalation of the West’s diplomatic break with Israel. International attention has shifted from Gaza to the West Bank, where settlers have repeatedly attacked Palestinian civilians while the Israeli military stood aside. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio criticised the British sanctions; however, the US Ambassador to Israel separately described West Bank settler attackers as “terrorists.”

Former Israeli generals and prime ministers have joined the international chorus denouncing settler violence, suggesting the rupture is more than performative. Israel’s deepening isolation from Western partners may affect its negotiating position on Lebanon and the ongoing ceasefire framework.

🏦 World · Finance (Update)

Update: ECB Rate Decision Tomorrow — +25bp to 2.65% Now Near-Certain

The European Central Bank meets Thursday for what markets and economists alike now treat as a foregone conclusion: a 25 basis-point hike to a policy rate of 2.65%, the second and likely final move in what may be the ECB’s shortest hiking campaign in 15 years. With inflation running above 3%, Goldman Sachs expects the vote to be unanimous. Prediction markets (Robinhood, Polymarket) price the hike at 99% probability. The ECB has signalled little appetite to go further after September, partly because long-term inflation expectations remain anchored — but the one-two punch of oil near $100 and bond yields rising is keeping pressure on the decision.

The rate path matters for Lebanon: higher European rates typically tighten dollar liquidity globally and raise the cost of any external refinancing. It also puts pressure on the EUR/USD rate — currently 1.161 — which affects the euro-denominated portion of any Lebanese government or corporate debt.

Reuters · Goldman Sachs · Morningstar · Polymarket
Why it matters to you

Tighter European rates sustain dollar borrowing costs for Lebanese entities and may delay any Eurobond restructuring deal timeline. Watch the ECB statement Thursday for any hint of a longer pause — that would be the first relief signal in months.

📉 World · Education

PISA 2026: Teenagers Are “Growing Dimmer” — 15-Year-Olds Now Testing at 14-Year-Old Levels

The OECD’s 2026 PISA results, released this week, show that today’s 15-year-olds are performing at levels typical of 14-year-olds a decade ago — and that the post-COVID recovery governments hoped for has not materialised. The Economist, leading with this story, says that rather than rebounding, cognitive test scores have continued declining: roughly a year’s worth of learning lost compared with pre-pandemic benchmarks, with no sign of stabilisation. The findings span reading, maths and science across dozens of countries. American scores in particular approached a 25-year low, the NYT separately confirmed.

Governments have spent heavily on devices and digital tools, but the data suggest screen time and disrupted schooling have had lasting effects on adolescent cognition. Separate Economist reporting notes that the number of US students in special education is rising sharply even as overall enrolment falls, adding cost pressure to already-strained state budgets.

☢️ World · Security

Economist: World Leaders Are Losing Their Fear of Nuclear War — A Dangerous Drift

The Economist’s lead editorial this week warns that Putin, Trump and Xi — who collectively control the most powerful nuclear arsenals on earth — are showing signs of losing the healthy terror of nuclear escalation that kept the Cold War from going hot. Russia has repeatedly invoked nuclear threats over Ukraine without consequence; the US-Iran war has normalised ballistic-missile exchanges in a region adjacent to nuclear-capable states; China has conducted live-fire exercises near Taiwan. The Economist argues this erosion of deterrence psychology is one of the most under-discussed security risks of the moment.

The editorial calls for renewed public and government focus on nuclear risk, noting that each of the three leaders has shown willingness to push further than their predecessors would have contemplated. The BRICS summit in New Delhi next week (Sept 12-13), bringing together Xi, Putin and Iran’s Pezeshkian, adds further texture to a world where the non-Western nuclear bloc is increasingly coordinated.

🤝 Africa · Nigeria Security

Nigeria Reportedly Entered Secret Ceasefire With Boko Haram Faction — Three Months of Relative Calm in Northeast

The Nigerian government has reportedly entered a secret ceasefire agreement with the Boko Haram faction led by Bakura Doro, with the truce holding quietly since June. Premium Times, which broke the story, says attacks attributed to the Bakura Doro faction have fallen significantly over the past three months, keeping parts of northeastern Nigeria relatively stable. The government has not acknowledged the deal publicly. Critically, the truce does not extend to ISWAP (the Islamic State West Africa Province), which remains active and continues to conduct operations in the Lake Chad basin and Borno State.

A quiet Bakura Doro reduces civilian casualties and gives the Nigerian military more bandwidth to concentrate on ISWAP. However, the secrecy of the arrangement — and the risk it collapses — means the northeast remains volatile for any infrastructure or project work.

Economist: Iran War Disrupting Hormuz Shipping Is Reviving Middle East Land Trade Routes

The Economist this week identifies a significant structural shift: with the Strait of Hormuz under sustained tanker attack and insurance costs for sea routes hitting record highs, Middle Eastern land trade corridors are making a comeback. The Turkey-Iraq-Syria-Levant overland routes — dormant for decades due to conflict — are seeing renewed interest from logistics operators, traders and governments. The Economist explicitly links this to a potential rejuvenation of regional commerce in the Middle East, noting that the disruption to sea routes is large enough to make overland economics competitive for the first time in a generation.

For Lebanon, this is a double-edged story: a functioning Beirut port and Tripoli corridor could benefit from redirected trade, but Lebanon’s own infrastructure and political fragility remain constraints. The Economist notes that the window for capturing this trade shift is narrow and rewards whoever moves infrastructure investment fastest.

Oil Near $100 + ECB Hike Tomorrow: A Tightening Vice on Lebanon’s Dollar Costs

Two converging forces are squeezing Lebanon’s already-fragile fiscal position today. Brent crude near $100 raises the cost of fuel imports and generator diesel across the country, where power cuts remain endemic and reliance on private generators is structural. Tomorrow’s near-certain ECB rate hike to 2.65% tightens global dollar liquidity further, increasing the cost of any external refinancing path for Lebanon’s $31 billion in defaulted Eurobonds. Goldman Sachs reiterated last week that Lebanese Eurobonds (BBI ~29.7) remain overvalued relative to realistic restructuring outcomes, and the IMF technical mission that resumed in Beirut last week has yet to produce a new programme framework.

The forensic audit of Banque du Liban by Alvarez & Marsal — tracing the reported $20 billion in missing reserves — continues in the background. Progress or revelations from that process remain the single most impactful potential positive catalyst for Lebanon’s IMF negotiating position.
