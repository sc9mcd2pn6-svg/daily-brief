Houthis / Yemen

Economist: How the Houthis built their war machine — “rebels in flipflops” now menacing Saudi Arabia

The Economist leads its Sunday edition with a deep investigation into how Yemen’s Houthi movement transformed from a ragtag insurgency into the most dangerous militia in the Middle East. Over a decade of control of northern Yemen, the Houthis — financed and armed by Iran but increasingly outgrowing that dependence — have built up a formidable arsenal of long-range drones, anti-ship missiles and ballistic missiles. In recent weeks they have struck Saudi Arabia repeatedly, including the first confirmed strikes on Riyadh since 2022. L’Orient Today’s Monday brief confirms the Riyadh strikes are the day’s lead security story.

The Economist traces three pillars of Houthi capability: Iranian weapons transfers (now supplemented by domestically assembled variants), battlefield experience from years of war against Saudi-backed Yemeni forces, and an intelligence network that has tracked Saudi air-defence gaps. Saudi Arabia, already strained by its rapprochement with Iran through China’s brokered deal, is now in talks to have Tehran call off the Houthis — a negotiation the Economist says Washington is watching closely ahead of any broader Iran deal.

Source: Economist Today (Nov 1); L’Orient Today (Nov 2)

Why it matters to you

Saudi Arabia is a key market context for Quasar and A&C — Gulf clients who have seen Houthi drone and missile strikes are asking harder questions about physical security and resilience. Any escalation that disrupts Gulf air traffic or infrastructure procurement cycles ripples into your pipeline timing. The Houthi Red Sea disruptions have also kept shipping costs elevated, affecting dollar-denominated equipment imports into Lebanon and Nigeria.

The New York Times publishes a major investigation into the systematic weakening of the US scientific research establishment under Trump’s second term. The piece documents NIH grant pipelines drying up, scientific advisory boards disbanded, senior FDA and CDC scientists walking out in protest, and a regulatory rollback that is exposing consumers to products previously kept off the market by evidence-based review (including Robert Kennedy Jr.’s peptide-injection access order last week).

The long-term consequence the NYT flags is a brain-drain that may take a decade to reverse — graduate students choosing Canada, the EU or industry; international researchers not renewing visas; and foundational research that underpins future US technological advantage being cut. China is increasing its research spending by 15% annually while the US cuts.

India

India’s “Cockroach” uprising — one of the biggest New Delhi protests yet expected this week as Modi faces grassroots revolt

The New York Times reports that mass protests are expected in New Delhi in the coming days — the latest in a string of demonstrations since summer that organisers say will be one of the largest yet. The “Cockroach Janta Party” youth movement, initially born from university exam paper leaks, has expanded into a demand for the resignation of Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar over alleged voter-roll manipulation. Police have detained thousands and cut mobile internet in previous crackdowns, which has intensified rather than dampened the movement.

Al Jazeera describes it as the most significant student uprising in India in a generation. Modi’s government has denied voter-roll manipulation and called demonstrators “anarchists” but has not publicly addressed their core demands. With state elections in Uttar Pradesh and Gujarat coming in 2027, the political stakes are rising for a party that has relied on youth and aspirational voters as core constituency.

Source: NYT Today’s Headlines (Nov 1); Al Jazeera (Oct 2026); NPR/WUNC

Vatican

Pope Leo departs for South America this Friday — 12-day tour, 10 cities; first major trip after lung surgery

Pope Leo XIV leaves this Friday November 6 for Uruguay, Argentina and Peru — his first major overseas trip and first visit to South America as pontiff. The Vatican confirmed the tour will proceed as planned despite the 71-year-old pope’s recent partial lung removal due to cancer; his doctors found no medical contraindications. The itinerary covers Uruguay (Nov 6–8), Argentina (Nov 8–11) and Peru (Nov 11–17), with stops in Lima, Chiclayo, Cusco and Pucallpa. The trip spans 31,000 km, 10 cities and roughly 50 events including 36 speeches, 12 flights and two helicopter transfers.

Peru is the centrepiece: Leo served as a missionary and bishop there for years before becoming pope, and the Vatican has dubbed the Lima leg a “papal homecoming.” The Economist notes the trip kicks off the week in which Americans also go to the polls and October’s US jobs data is published Friday — a week with outsized geopolitical weight.

Source: Reuters; Buenos Aires Times; Catholic Weekly; Economist Today (Nov 1)

Lebanon Economy

Financial Gap Law: IMF won’t back cabinet’s version — parliament vote still unscheduled; IMF deal in the balance

Lebanon’s path to an IMF rescue programme remains blocked by the Financial Gap Law. Cabinet passed a draft in December 2025 by 13 votes to 9, referring it to parliament; the IMF then publicly stated it would not back the cabinet version and called for revisions to the loss-allocation methodology, specifically the timeline for banks to reach capital adequacy targets. As of late October 2026, no parliament vote is scheduled. Finance Minister Yassine Jaber met the IMF’s Beirut representative in August and confirmed an IMF programme remains the top priority, but the IMF’s call for revisions means the current draft cannot unlock a programme.

The banking restructuring law, separately, was called a “major step” by the IMF when parliament passed it in August 2026. But the Financial Gap Law — which determines who bears the $72 billion in losses from Lebanon’s banking collapse — is the last remaining precondition the IMF is waiting on. BdL Governor’s year-end IMF breakthrough target is now very tight.

Source: L’Orient Today; Enterprise AM; LBCI; Blominvest (Aug–Oct 2026)

Why it matters to you

An IMF programme unlock would immediately improve dollar liquidity, client confidence and payment behaviour in Beirut. Without it, large project clients remain cautious on capital spending and payment terms stay stretched. The end-of-year timeline is the window: if parliament votes before December, the IMF could move in Q1 2027 — which would change the bidding environment for Quasar’s spring pipeline.

Act on this: Track the November parliamentary session calendar; flag immediately if the Financial Gap Law gets a committee date.

Real Estate Beirut

Beirut 2026 apartment prices: Achrafieh up 10%+, Downtown luxury holds — Ras Beirut recovering unevenly after April war damage

L’Orient Today and Ramco Real Estate Advisers have published a district-by-district breakdown of Beirut apartment prices in 2026. Achrafieh has seen the strongest recovery: new-development prices rose more than 10% since the June ceasefire framework, driven by returning diaspora demand and a shortage of quality stock below $500K for sub-100m² units. Downtown Beirut’s ultra-luxury segment has proved resilient — consultants report that premium pricing has held despite overall market uncertainty and tight dollar liquidity, with buyers coming mainly from Gulf and French-Lebanese diaspora with clean cash.

Ras Beirut is the laggard. Several neighbourhoods were directly hit during the April 8 bombing, and while transaction activity has resumed, prices in affected streets remain below their 2025 peak. The National (October 2026) reports new apartment projects still launching in Beirut despite the war and weak economy — developers betting on continued diaspora demand regardless of political resolution.

Source: L’Orient Today / Ramco (Oct 2026); The National (Oct 2, 2026)

Why it matters to you

If you hold in Achrafieh, the 10%+ recovery means this is an opportune moment to re-assess valuations for any refinancing or exit decision. The Downtown luxury market is the most liquid right now for large assets. Avoid rushing decisions on Ras Beirut holdings — the recovery is real but uneven and tied to the security outlook.
