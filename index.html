Paramount/Warner Bros. $111 Billion Mega-Merger Clears Final Hurdle — David Ellison Controls CNN, CBS & HBO

Paramount has settled the last antitrust lawsuit blocking its $111 billion acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery, reached with a group of state attorneys general led by California. The deal will create a media behemoth controlling CNN, CBS News, HBO, HBO Max, Warner Bros. films, Discovery, and dozens of streaming services. In exchange for the states’ consent, Paramount agreed to establish an independent board of journalists to protect the editorial independence of CNN and CBS News, distribute 30 films theatrically per year, and spend an additional $1.5 billion on US film production over five years.

David Ellison, CEO of Paramount and an ally of President Trump, will become one of the most powerful figures in global entertainment. The NYT notes that the deal — in its “Evening” edition lead — raises serious questions about the independence of CNN and CBS News given Ellison’s proximity to the White House. EU Invites Canada to Become Its First “Associate Member” — Macron and Carney Meet on French Island off Newfoundland

In a symbolically charged meeting on Saint-Pierre-et-Miquelon — France’s tiny island territory off the coast of Canada — President Macron and Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney formalized a partnership framework after the European Union offered Canada the status of “associate member.” This is the first time the EU has offered such a designation to a non-European country, designed to give Canada privileged access to EU defence industrial co-operation, economic partnerships and diplomatic coordination without full membership.

The NYT describes the meeting as the most visible expression yet of a trans-Atlantic realignment driven by Trump’s tariff war and America’s retreat from multilateralism. Canada, under Carney, has moved aggressively to diversify away from US dependency, signing a $50 billion EU-Canada green industrial pact in July. For Macron, the deal extends French and EU soft power to a G7 economy — and signals to Washington that transatlantic institutions will reconstitute themselves around America rather than waiting for it.

China’s Youth Unemployment Has Become “A Way of Life” — Millions Pretending to Work to Keep Up Appearances

The Economist’s latest reporting on China’s internal economy finds that years of persistently high youth unemployment — the official rate was 18.8% in August — are fundamentally reshaping Chinese society. Young graduates increasingly describe unemployment not as a temporary setback but as a normalised condition: staying at home, receiving support from parents, and in some cases fabricating a work routine to maintain the appearance of employment. The phenomenon is generating a new vocabulary on Chinese social media and accelerating demographic pressures on a society already facing a declining birth rate.

The Economist notes that this social reality sits in sharp tension with China’s AI ambitions: Beijing is simultaneously claiming technological supremacy while a generation of educated young people cannot find work in the economy that technology is supposedly powering. As Xi arrives in Washington, the domestic economic fragility behind the diplomatic confidence is the context that shapes his actual negotiating room.

An IMF mission led by Ernesto Ramírez Rígo concluded a four-day visit to Beirut (September 15–18), and issued its formal assessment: parliament’s passage of amendments to the bank resolution law is “a major step” that aligns Lebanon’s bank restructuring framework with international standards. This is the reform the IMF has insisted on as a prerequisite for any formal lending arrangement, and its passage — following PM Salam’s government pushing it through a parliament that resisted for five years — marks genuine progress. Daily Beirut and BLOMINVEST both confirmed the IMF reaffirmed its support for Lebanon’s reform agenda.

The economic backdrop remains severe: Lebanon’s activity is expected to contract significantly in 2026, inflation remains in double digits, and the current account deficit is widening on higher energy costs and war-related infrastructure damage. The IMF called for a more ambitious 2026 budget than the cabinet has submitted and pushed for a medium-term fiscal framework. PM Salam met the IMF delegation and reiterated his government’s commitment to reforms. The gap between what the IMF requires and what is achievable politically is narrowing, but no formal programme has yet been agreed.

The Economist’s lead financial analysis warns that the housing market faces a harder test this cycle than it did in 2022–23.
