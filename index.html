Update: Saudi Arabia Running Out of Options as Pipeline Stays Down for Weeks

Saudi Arabia is scrambling to reroute millions of barrels of oil after drone strikes knocked out the 1,200km East-West pipeline, and repair teams now say the damage will keep the conduit offline for weeks rather than days. The kingdom confirmed it has also come under fresh attack from Iran-backed Houthis who have tightened their grip on the Bab el-Mandeb Strait — the southern exit from the Red Sea — while Iran continues to threaten Hormuz in the Gulf. With both critical export routes blocked or contested, Saudi Arabia, the world’s largest oil exporter, is effectively landlocked in its own geography.

Brent crude opened Thursday at $108.51, reflecting a more than 4% rise since the pipeline shutdown, and traders warn of a push toward $115–120 if disruptions persist into October. An extended outage would constitute a severe global energy crisis; Asian buyers, particularly South Korea, Japan, and India, are the most exposed to rerouting costs and delays. The Saudi energy ministry has activated emergency reserves but says they cannot compensate indefinitely for a full pipeline stoppage.

EU Invites Canada to Become Its First-Ever ‘Associate Member’ as US-Led Order Splinters

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen used her State of the Union address in Strasbourg on Wednesday to extend an offer of “associate membership” of the European Union to Canada — a legal status that has never existed before. Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney was present and received a standing ovation from European lawmakers. The offer proposes a sweeping “Alliance for the Future” covering critical minerals, defence industrial capacity, AI and compute infrastructure, energy security, space, and financial services. The framing was explicit: with US-Canada and US-EU ties badly damaged by Trump-era tariffs and confrontational trade policy, Europe and Canada are building an alternative bloc.

Key questions remain unanswered — most critically, whether Canada would gain any voting rights in EU institutions and how “associate member” would differ from CETA, the existing free-trade agreement. Critics in Canada note that parliament has not been consulted and that trade rules would need to be renegotiated. Nevertheless, the symbolic and strategic weight of the announcement is considerable: it signals that Western allies are actively restructuring their alliances around a non-US axis.

Oil Above $100 Sparks a Wave of Global Fuel Protests From Asia to Latin America

Rising fuel prices driven by the Iran war are triggering the first major wave of social unrest across the developing world. In Indonesia, cooking-gas shortages and petrol rationing have fuelled protests in Makassar and other cities; in Guatemala, truckers and Indigenous activists blockaded highways and two police were wounded in clashes; Syria has seen burning-tyre roadblocks as electricity blackouts worsen; Bangladesh is rationing. Across these markets, pump prices have risen 30–60% since the Iran war erupted in early 2026 as import costs and shipping disruptions compound.

Governments are facing a stark choice between absorbing subsidy costs — expensive given already-strained budgets — or allowing price pass-through that risks further unrest. Guatemala’s congress is debating a price-cap law. Indonesia has invoked emergency petroleum regulations. The unrest shows no sign of abating as long as Brent remains above $100 and the Bab el-Mandeb corridor stays contested. The NYT flags this as one of the clearest signs that the Iran war’s economic costs are now being felt globally by ordinary households.

Cabinet Approves Key Banking Reform Amendments — Salam’s Government Pushes for FATF Compliance Before Year-End

Lebanon’s cabinet approved amendments to two key articles of the banking restructuring law in its latest session, after a working session between President Aoun, Prime Minister Salam, and Central Bank Governor Karim Saeed. The amendments strengthen regulatory oversight of commercial bank restructuring, improve anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorism-financing frameworks, and align Lebanese legislation with IMF and FATF requirements. Governor Saeed presented the compliance obligations in detail, stressing the need to licence and supervise financial institutions before year-end to avoid FATF grey-listing, which would further complicate international correspondent banking.

The broader banking reform bill — distributing the 2019 crisis losses among banks, the state, and depositors, with depositor access up to $100,000 over four years — remains the central IMF precondition for any programme. The cabinet amendments signal that the Salam government is moving more decisively than its predecessors, though parliament must still ratify the full law. IMF had previously flagged Lebanon’s 2026 budget as “not ambitious enough” and the cash economy as still 45.7% of GDP.
