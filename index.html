Pentagon Drafts 3-Day Iran Strike Plan — Trump Has Repeatedly Vetoed It

The US military has drawn up detailed options for a three-day campaign against Iran’s missile infrastructure, according to the New York Times, but President Trump has vetoed the plans multiple times. Officials say he is determined not to restart major combat before November’s midterm elections — a public commitment that analysts say has handed the Houthis and Iran’s remaining proxies a window of effective impunity through the autumn. CENTCOM planners have separately weighed strikes on energy infrastructure as a broader option, and the options remain formally ready to execute.

Gulf states are in an awkward position: Saudi Arabia and the UAE have suffered repeated attacks on civilian airports and infrastructure yet their principal security guarantor has publicly declined to respond. Insurance premiums on Gulf aviation and energy assets continue to rise, and tanker re-routing via the Cape of Good Hope — now standard practice — adds 10–14 days to transit times, pushing freight costs higher for all Gulf-dependent supply chains. A post-midterm posture change could dramatically accelerate the tempo of regional operations.

⚓️ World · Iran / Economy

Economist: America’s Blockade Is Breaking Iran’s Economy — But Not Yet Its Regime

This week’s Economist leads with a detailed assessment of the US economic campaign against Iran. The IMF projects a 6.1% GDP contraction for 2026, with inflation nearing 69%. The US Treasury’s “Operation Economic Outcast,” launched in August, expanded this month to sanction Iran’s auto sector — Iran Khodro and SAIPA, which together account for over 90% of domestic car production — and the state railway, cutting off the overland alternatives Iran adopted when the naval blockade shut seaborne trade. The Economist’s correspondent asks whether economic pain at this scale will push Tehran toward a ceasefire but notes the regime is using nationalism and external blame to stay resilient for now.

Secondary effects extend beyond Iran: the sustained blockade keeps an elevated oil risk premium across the Gulf, contributes to high global LNG prices as Europe scrambles for alternatives, and disrupts supply chains for dozens of countries including Lebanon and Nigeria. Treasury Secretary Bessent described the measures as “unprecedented in the history of economic isolation.”

Economist (anchor, “Counting chickens in Tehran,” 9 Oct 2026) · ARY News · WUSF/NPR · Reuters
Why it matters to you

An Iran under maximum economic pressure is more likely to either capitulate or lash out. A lash-out scenario means another Gulf security escalation and a possible sharp oil spike that drives Lebanese energy costs higher and raises Quasar Nigeria’s dollar-denominated import costs. The 69% inflation figure also signals extreme stress for any Iranian supply partners or distribution contacts you deal with.

📈 World · Finance / Wealth

Economist: Shares Are the New Bricks and Mortar — Property Loses Its Status as Global Wealth Vehicle

Today’s Economist cover identifies a landmark structural shift in global household wealth: owning a home is losing its status as the primary path to financial security, especially for younger generations. The piece argues that stocks now decisively outperform property on a risk-adjusted, long-run basis, and that young people across most developed economies can no longer realistically achieve housing wealth given current prices. While house prices survived the last interest-rate rise, the Economist warns this time may be different — a sustained higher-rate environment is biting deeper into both affordability and the investment case for real estate.

The implications span markets from London to Beirut. If global investors — including diaspora savers — shift their mental model away from “property = wealth” toward “stocks = wealth,” demand for high-end residential real estate as an investment class could soften materially over a 3–5 year horizon. Prime, supply-constrained locations are more resilient; secondary markets face sharper corrections. Data-centre real estate is the notable counter-trend, with institutional demand for that asset class continuing to surge globally.

Economist (anchor, “Why shares are the new bricks and mortar,” 9 Oct 2026)
Why it matters to you

Your prime Beirut holdings are in a supply-constrained market and hold value well, but diaspora demand — which drives a significant share of Beirut prime transactions — could soften if the savings narrative shifts to equities. Hold positions are fine; for new acquisitions factor slower capital appreciation into the model. The data-centre real estate counter-trend is worth watching for any commercial strategy.

🏠 World · Spain / Politics

Spain’s Housing Crisis Spirals After Maricarmen Death — Snap Election Called for 29 November

Spain’s national housing crisis has reached political breaking point. Weeks of protests following the eviction and subsequent death of 87-year-old pensioner Maricarmen Abascal culminated in mass marches across Madrid and 50+ cities on October 3, drawing an estimated 70,000–500,000 people (authorities vs organisers). Parliament voted down PM Sánchez’s emergency tenant-protection package 178–172 after Catalan separatist party Junts sided with the opposition, prompting Sánchez to call a snap election for November 29. Polls point to a PP+Vox coalition, signalling a sharp rightward shift and the likely end of the government’s housing reform agenda.

The protests draw comparisons to Spain’s 15-M movement of 2011 and underscore that housing affordability — driven by tourism pressure, short-term rentals, and static supply — has become an existential political issue across southern Europe. The NYT frames Spain’s unrest as part of a continent-wide housing crisis reshaping politics from London to Lisbon.

Update: French Protests Enter Week 3 — Violence Escalates, Universities Now Blockaded

France’s student uprising over dilapidated schools and unfilled teaching posts has entered a third week, spreading from secondary schools to universities. Over 6,000 people have been arrested since mid-September — 85% of them minors. In a disturbing escalation in Lens, a 15-year-old lost a hand after picking up what appeared to be a stun grenade near a protest; the police unit involved is under investigation by the inspectorate-general. The Union Syndicale Lycéenne claimed 1,800 schools were closed or blockaded on October 6, and university buildings in Paris and Lyon are now being occupied.

PM Lecornu pledged to fill vacant teaching posts and address urgent building conditions, but unions say the measures fall far short and no structural concessions have been made. The movement is now threatening to merge with Labour and union campaigns ahead of an October 12 planned national strike. Macron’s approval has fallen 7 points since protests began, and commentators note the crisis is already shaping the 2027 presidential race. The Economist explicitly links France’s political paralysis to sovereign bond market risk.

Agence Nova · ITV News · Bluewin/AFP · Economist (anchor) · Le Monde · Malaysia Mail
Why it matters to you

French political instability directly bears on French sovereign risk, which in turn affects euro-zone financial flows that reach Lebanese banks and commercial credit lines. 📈 Lebanon · Economy / IMF

IMF Missions Arrive Monday; Lebanon GDP to Contract 6.4% in 2026 — Financial Gap Law Still Without a Vote

IMF teams arrive in Beirut on Monday October 13 for the critical round of discussions that will determine whether Lebanon can sign a new staff-level agreement. The backdrop is sobering: the World Bank projects Lebanon’s GDP to shrink by more than 6% in 2026 — PM Salam has cited 6.4% — reversing much of the modest 4.2% rebound of 2025 and extending a cumulative collapse of roughly 50% since 2019. The Financial Gap Law, which must pass parliament before any IMF programme can be finalised, still has no scheduled vote, with Hezbollah and the Lebanese Forces having voted against it in cabinet, and Banque du Liban mounting sustained resistance to the loss-allocation provisions.

PM Salam met US Secretary of State Rubio in Washington this week amid stalled Lebanon-Israel peace talks, underscoring that political and economic pressure tracks are tightly intertwined. Finance Minister Jaber says he remains optimistic about a near-term staff-level deal, but the IMF’s own tone is less bullish and no timeline has been set. Without the law and a deal, Lebanon’s ability to attract foreign direct investment remains severely constrained into 2027.

Update: Manchester City Files Appeal Against 114-Charge Verdict — Punishment Still Undecided

Manchester City have formally filed their appeal against the independent commission’s finding that they breached Premier League financial rules on 114 of 115 charges. The club’s statement called the verdict “unsafe” and alleged “clear material errors of law, principle and fact.” The appeal does not pause proceedings: a separate punishment panel is due to rule on sanctions, which could include a massive points deduction, transfer ban, or — in the most extreme outcome — expulsion from the Premier League. The FA has separately indicated it may take additional disciplinary action.

Premier League clubs are divided on how severe the punishment should be, with smaller clubs pushing for the harshest measures while others warn of systemic damage from expelling one of football’s most commercially valuable clubs. The case is unprecedented in English football history; no timeline for the punishment ruling has been set.
