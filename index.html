The Economist: World Faces an Almighty LNG Crunch — Iran War Wiped Out 20% of Global Supply; European Winter at Risk

The Economist’s Sunday edition leads with a stark warning: the war in Iran has triggered the largest peacetime disruption to natural-gas markets since the 1970s. When Iranian drones struck Qatar’s Ras Laffan LNG complex in early March, roughly 20% of global LNG supply was taken off the market overnight. European natural gas futures jumped over 40% on day one. Buyers of LNG who were hoping for a mild northern hemisphere winter are “praying”; if autumn is cold and Hormuz harassment continues, the Economist warns a price shock could rival or exceed 2022’s energy crisis, when prices hit record highs following the Ukraine invasion. Delta Air Lines reported Friday that adjusted fuel costs rose 77% year-on-year, an early corporate signal of the cumulative damage.

The Economist notes a second, longer-term consequence: war-driven LNG investment is accelerating, which means a significant glut could follow the crunch in three to five years once new terminals come online. For now, though, the short-term pressure is brutal, and Lebanon — whose power grid depends heavily on imported fuel — is directly exposed. The LNG crunch is also reshaping global shipping routes, benefiting Gulf producers who can substitute pipeline for LNG.

Lebanon Banking Reform: Parliament Adopted Restructuring Law but Loss Allocation — the $72 Billion Question — Heads to Vote Mid-October

L’Orient Today reports that while Lebanon’s Parliament formally adopted amendments to the Bank Restructuring Law in August 2026 — a step long demanded by the IMF — the law still does not answer the fundamental question: who bears the estimated $72 billion in accumulated losses among the state, Banque du Liban, commercial banks and depositors? That decision, the “Financial Gap Law,” is where the real political fight lies. Parliamentary Finance Committee chair Ibrahim Kanaan confirmed in September that no vote on the Financial Gap Law would happen before mid-October. BDL Governor Wassim Souhaid has been actively lobbying against any provision that imposes losses on the central bank, creating a serious institutional obstacle.

The IMF has stated clearly that a credible loss-allocation framework is a prerequisite for any staff-level agreement. Without it, Lebanon’s Eurobonds — currently trading at roughly 25 cents on the dollar — will remain distressed. L’Orient Today’s assessment is sobering: the parliamentary compromise that passed the restructuring law “leaves key issues unresolved” and risks becoming a repeat of Lebanon’s long history of passing reform outlines without implementing their substance.
