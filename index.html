AI selloff routs Wall Street — S&P 500 and Dow enter correction after worst week since 2020

The S&P 500 closed Friday at 6,910, down 3.7% on the day and off 8.6% for the week — its seventh consecutive losing session and deepest correction since the 2025 trade-war rout. The Dow Jones fell to 48,147 (−1.9%), while the Nasdaq erased all of its 2026 gains, closing at 22,618 (−5.3% on the day). The selloff accelerated as multiple AI and technology concerns converged: Anthropic’s ANT shares gave back most of Thursday’s debut gains on concerns that the $1.7 trillion valuation is unsustainable; the Nvidia–China chip scandal continued to weigh; and broader questions about AI profitability resurfaced.

NYT coverage described the week as driven by “fear of AI pitfalls” and the environmental and infrastructure costs of data centres, adding that political opposition to data-centre proliferation had helped Democrats win key midterm seats. The last time the S&P 500 endured a correction of this depth was March 2025 during peak trade-war anxiety. The Nasdaq’s wipe-out of 2026 gains is the most significant calendar-year reversal since December 2022.

UK and EU sign ‘landmark’ Brussels deal — food checks, youth mobility and emissions trading; first real Brexit reset

Three agreements were signed at the second annual UK–EU summit in Brussels on 20 November: a Sanitary and Phytosanitary deal cutting food and agricultural checks at the border; a youth mobility scheme allowing up to 100,000 young people annually from each side to live, study and work across the Channel for up to three years; and a linking of the UK and EU emissions trading systems from 2028. It is the most significant British re-engagement with the European Union since the 2016 Brexit vote. All three accords require parliamentary ratification on both sides and do not take effect until 2028.

Prime Minister Andy Burnham called the deals a step toward “rebuilding bridges” and said government modelling projects a 0.5% GDP boost. The EU’s foreign policy chief cautioned that “Brussels is still traumatised by Brexit” and that re-entry talks remain off the table. Burnham separately confirmed that a wider review of the long-term UK–EU relationship will begin in 2027, with a report expected in 2028.

Trump threatens ‘tremendous tax’ on US firms building data centres abroad — frames AI as national-security race with China

At his first news conference since returning from the APEC Shenzhen summit, President Trump threatened to impose a “tremendous tax” or other penalties on American companies building AI data centres outside the United States. Trump framed the proposal as necessary to maintain supremacy over China in the AI race, while acknowledging the idea had not yet been “previewed to my people” and remained in its infancy. He promised to “fight very hard for data centers” domestically. No formal executive order or legislative vehicle was announced.

The backdrop is growing Democratic opposition to domestic data-centre expansion — concerns about power consumption, water use and local environmental impact helped drive Democrats’ midterm gains. Tech companies have yet to respond formally to Trump’s remarks. If enacted, a tax on overseas AI infrastructure would represent a major shift in US technology export policy and could force hyperscalers to reconsider their global build-out strategies in Southeast Asia, Europe and Africa.

Lagos data-centre boom hits energy wall — costs up 64% in 2026, grid supplies just 3,000–4,000 MW nationwide

Lagos is racing toward 250 MW of data-centre capacity by 2030, up from 78.6 MW today. Airtel is building a 38 MW hyperscale facility at Eko Atlantic; Digital Parks Africa opened a new carrier-neutral site in September 2026; and MTN’s Dabengwa centre in Ikeja is in its first phase at $150 million. The CBN data-localisation deadline of January 2027 is adding further urgency, pushing 90% of Nigerian fintechs to migrate off foreign cloud. But the sector faces a severe energy constraint: electricity costs have surged 64.1% since January 2026, while national grid output hovers between 3,000 and 4,000 MW against demand — leaving every serious data-centre operator reliant on on-site diesel generation for near-100% uptime.

TechCabal and Nairametrics both flag grid reliability as the single biggest bottleneck to sector growth, noting that off-site renewables are the medium-term answer but that lead times make them irrelevant to the January 2027 wave. Premium Times confirmed that Nigeria’s power minister acknowledged this month that half of installed 13,000 MW never reaches consumers. The pipeline of new builds is nonetheless the fastest in West Africa.

French students join Nov 20 European walkout — tuition, housing and job scarcity fuel mobilisation separate from budget strikes

French student unions joined the pan-European day of action on 20 November, staging walkouts and demonstrations in Paris, Lyon and Bordeaux focused on university tuition costs, student housing shortages and graduate unemployment. The mobilisation is distinct from the November 17 general strike over purchasing power — it draws from younger cohorts with different grievances: the cost of a university room in Paris now exceeds €900/month, and graduate underemployment stands at 22%. Several thousand students gathered at the Place de la République before marching toward Sciences Po.

The student protests add a new front for PM Lecornu, who survived a no-confidence vote on 19 November but governs without a majority. France’s GDP contracted 0.1% in Q3; with the main budget still unresolved, analysts warn of a technical government shutdown risk in January if the Assembly does not pass a provisional funding measure. The 2027 presidential race tightens: Marine Le Pen leads Lecornu in polls at 34–36% vs. 28%; a centrist “above politics” candidate is being actively explored.

Lebanon IMF deal shifts to Q1 2027 — Jaber confirms first-quarter deadline; World Bank projects −6.4% GDP contraction in 2026

Finance Minister Yassine Jaber confirmed this week that Lebanon’s target is to sign a formal IMF programme in the first quarter of 2027, following the breakdown of November talks. The IMF concluded its Beirut mission saying “further work is needed to reach agreement on a strategy for effective and durable restructuring” of the banking sector — specifically that the August banking resolution law “needs further refinement” and that the Financial Gap Law, which addresses how banking-sector losses are distributed across depositors, banks and the state, has not been voted on. The National reported the finance minister remains confident a deal can be structured before the parliamentary recess.

The economic backdrop is worsening: the World Bank’s November Lebanon Economic Monitor projects GDP will contract 6.4% in 2026, the third consecutive year of contraction, and notes that just $4.2 million of a June 2025 $250-million World Bank infrastructure grant has been disbursed. Lebanese Eurobonds have held firm despite the stalemate, per L’Orient Today, sustained by speculative positioning on a future restructuring rather than any fundamental improvement. UNIFIL’s December 31 mandate expiry remains unresolved, with Hezbollah disarmament talks stalled.

Sources: Arab News (Nov 2026); The National (Nov 2026); L’Orient Today; World Bank Lebanon Economic Monitor
