Press Freedom

Russia silences Novaya Gazeta for good — 33 years of defiant reporting ends

Novaya Gazeta, Russia’s most prominent independent newspaper and home to Nobel Peace Prize laureate Dmitry Muratov, held its final editorial meeting and vacated its central Moscow office on 9 November. Editor Sergei Sokolov said the paper was “killed” by the state: its website was blocked, its print licence revoked, its presses seized, and an editor placed under house arrest. The paper had won a Nobel Prize in 2021 for courage in covering the Chechen wars, Kremlin corruption, and the Ukraine invasion.

Some former staff who had already fled Russia launched Novaya Gazeta Europe, which Moscow has since designated an “undesirable organisation.” The Kremlin called the closure routine market turnover. The NYT described the moment as the lights going out on 33 years of journalism under an increasingly repressive Russia.

Source: The New York Times (11 Nov); Reuters (via The Star Malaysia, 9 Nov); Kyiv Independent; CPJ; BNN Bloomberg

AUKUS sub base opens in Western Australia — first US naval base abroad in 53 years

The Economist reports on the opening of America’s first new submarine base outside US territory since 1971, built on Australia’s west coast as part of the AUKUS pact. The facility — at HMAS Stirling near Perth — will host US Virginia-class nuclear submarines and serve as the foundation for Australia’s own SSN-AUKUS programme, with the first Australian submarines due in the mid-2030s. The move signals a permanent shift of US naval focus toward the Indo-Pacific and is the most tangible infrastructure commitment of the AUKUS alliance.

China has condemned the base as destabilising. The Economist notes that AUKUS “is beginning to make waves on Australia’s west coast” and that the facility has reshuffled the strategic calculus from the South China Sea to the Indian Ocean.

Source: The Economist (11 Nov)

Lebanon Economy

IMF mission in Beirut hits a wall: Central Bank seeks carve-outs the Fund will not accept

The IMF technical mission now meeting in Beirut is facing a core impasse over the Financial Gap Law, which must pass before any IMF programme can begin. The Fund insists the law must maintain a strict hierarchy of claims — shareholders absorb losses first, then the state — but Banque du Liban is pushing for carve-outs that would effectively protect its own balance sheet. Meanwhile, the Banking Resolution Law passed in August was referred to the Constitutional Council by President Aoun, and its fate remains uncertain. The convergence of an active IMF mission, a blocked law, and a central bank seeking exceptions makes this a defining week for the reform programme.

Lebanon Security

Israel reportedly weighing “grey line” partial pullback in south Lebanon as UNIFIL clock ticks

L’Orient-Le Jour reports that after the “yellow line,” Israel is now considering a “grey line” approach — a partial reduction of its buffer-zone footprint in occupied south Lebanese territory without a full withdrawal. This would be a tactical compromise: keeping a residual presence to prevent Hezbollah re-armament while reducing the political cost of open-ended occupation as UNIFIL’s December 31 expiry nears. The Lebanese army has accelerated deployments in Nabatieh under EU-brokered talks, but Israel says disarmament obligations remain far short of commitments.

The EU security mission (EUSML) approved in October has a training mandate, not a deployment in the south, so the gap left by UNIFIL’s departure will not be filled by European boots on the ground. The situation remains fluid and volatile; a miscalculation in the south could restart hostilities.

Source: L’Orient-Le Jour; The National; Euronews; Al Jazeera

Why it matters to you

A stable south Lebanon — even a partial Israeli pullback — would unlock reconstruction contracts and security-system upgrades across the border zone. If hostilities restart, construction halts and insurance costs spike. Keep southern Lebanese projects on hold pending the UNIFIL expiry outcome in late December.
