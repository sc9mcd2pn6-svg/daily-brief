Update — Brent rises for 4th consecutive day above $91; Trump says “no talks” with Iran as stalemate hardens

Oil markets have extended their post-ceasefire-expiry rally to a fourth consecutive session, with Brent crude trading above $91 per barrel after adding approximately 4.5% across the four-day run. Any hope of near-term diplomatic movement was foreclosed on Tuesday when President Trump stated explicitly that there are “no talks ongoing with Tehran” — making clear that the expiry of the 60-day MOU has not produced a quiet back-channel but a genuine standoff. Iran continues its policy of demanding that commercial vessels route through Iranian-controlled lanes rather than established shipping lanes, and attacking those that refuse, while the US maintains its naval blockade posture. The USS George Washington carrier group is still in transit and has not reached the Arabian Sea, leaving US naval presence below its preferred level at the moment of maximum tension. Shipping traffic through the Strait of Hormuz remains severely curtailed, with Asian fuel shortages worsening.

🇭🇺 World · Europe & Hungary

Hungary’s András Baka sworn in as president today — post-Orbán era takes institutional shape

András Baka, 73 — a former chief justice of Hungary’s Supreme Court who was ousted by Orbán-era legislation and subsequently won a landmark case against Hungary at the European Court of Human Rights — was formally sworn in as Hungary’s president on August 19. His election by parliament on August 11, supported by Prime Minister Péter Magyar’s Tisza Party supermajority, ended a 16-year run during which Fidesz controlled every major Hungarian institution. Baka said Hungary cannot be built on “vengeance” but requires both justice and restraint; the symbolism of a man victimised by the Orbán system now heading the state is deliberate. Magyar’s government has pledged to reform Hungary’s constitutional court, judiciary and public media, and to restore the country’s status as a full-functioning EU member — a shift that will gradually alter the EU’s internal balance and remove a key spoiler in European policy decisions on Ukraine, sanctions and enlargement.

📉 World · US Markets

US equities fall for third session: S&P 500 −0.7%, Nasdaq −1.3% as oil and bond yields squeeze together

US stock markets closed lower for a third consecutive session on Tuesday August 18, with the S&P 500 falling 0.7%, the Dow Jones Industrial Average dipping 0.2% and the Nasdaq composite sinking 1.3% — each index moving further from last week’s record highs. The dual headwind of elevated oil (Brent above $91) and long-term Treasury yields extending to multi-year highs is creating a classic inflation-shadow compression: higher energy costs feed into inflation expectations, which push long-end yields up, which discount equity valuations. Technology stocks, which led this year’s earlier rally, were disproportionately hit through the yield channel. Federal Reserve meeting minutes from the July session are due Wednesday August 20; any hawkish signal there will reinforce market pricing of one to two rate hikes before year-end — a view that has already shifted sharply from the cuts expected at the start of 2026.

🕊️ World · Gaza

Gaza disarmament deadline passes with no progress; Hamas calls Israel’s bluff; 1.8 million still displaced

The 14-day deadline set by the US-backed Board of Peace for implementing the July 30 Hamas disarmament agreement has expired without meaningful movement, leaving the roadmap in legal limbo. Hamas negotiator Ghazi Hamad reiterated that the group will not begin any decommissioning process until Israel halts attacks and withdraws forces; Netanyahu’s office countered that no withdrawal would occur before full disarmament — a sequencing deadlock with no agreed mediator to break it. A Chatham House analysis published this week argues that Hamas accepted the roadmap precisely because it calculated Israel would refuse to meet its preconditions, making the disarmament offer a political move to expose Israeli intransigence rather than a genuine security commitment. The humanitarian picture inside Gaza remains dire: only 35% of the daily aid trucks allocated under the October 2025 ceasefire have actually entered, and 1.8 million people remain displaced and aid-dependent. The Board of Peace has not announced an extension or consequence for the missed deadline.

🇫🇷 France
🇫🇷 France · Diplomacy

France to expel two Iranian diplomats in coming days — Paris-Tehran standoff over embassy staff arrests escalates

French Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot announced on August 18 that France will expel two Iranian diplomats currently posted in Paris “in the coming days” — a direct reciprocal response to Iran’s decision to prevent two French diplomats from returning to the Tehran embassy. The confrontation began on July 19 when Iranian intelligence agents “deliberately assaulted” and detained two French embassy staff members in Tehran without formal charge; France protested vigorously and Iran subsequently declared both diplomats persona non grata. France’s own expulsion move marks a formal rupture in what had been a carefully managed distance between Paris and the US-Iran military standoff: France had backed the Hormuz coalition forces without directly targeting Iranian diplomatic channels. The expulsion also complicates any future French role as a diplomatic bridge to Tehran — a role France had preserved through months of the crisis — and signals that Paris has concluded that role is no longer viable.

🏗️ Africa · Data Centres & Investment

Africa data centre demand set to surge five-fold by 2030, requiring $10–20bn in fresh investment — Lagos identified as continent’s primary hub

Knight Frank’s Africa Report 2026/27, published this week and covered by Nairametrics on August 17, projects that Africa’s data centre demand could increase three to five times by 2030, requiring between $10 billion and $20 billion in fresh capital investment across the continent. Africa’s operational data centre capacity already surpassed 500 MW as of July 2026, with a further 890 MW in the development pipeline — but this is projected to fall well short of the demand driven by AI adoption, cloud services and Africa’s expanding fintech sector. Nigeria hosts 14 of the continent’s 17 operational data centres, with Lagos specifically positioned as the primary hub; mid-size data centres now carry build costs of $500 million to $2 billion each, meaning even a conservative buildout of 5–10 new Lagos facilities alone represents multi-billion-dollar investment. The Knight Frank report is the most authoritative external quantification yet of the scale of Africa’s coming digital infrastructure wave.

🏦 Africa · Nigeria & Fintech

CBN opens regulatory sandbox for virtual assets and fintechs through August 31 — Nigeria sharpens digital finance governance

The Central Bank of Nigeria launched the second cohort of its Regulatory Sandbox Programme on August 12, with applications open through August 31, 2026. The sandbox specifically targets virtual asset operators, fintech companies, financial institutions and technology firms seeking to operate innovative digital products under controlled regulatory conditions before the CBN finalises full licensing frameworks. The move is distinct from the January 2027 data localisation mandate (already reported): where the localisation order drives physical data centre demand, the sandbox is about formalising the legal and compliance layer that will govern the sector long-term. Nigeria’s fintech sector is on track to contribute an estimated $6 billion to GDP in 2026 — the largest fintech economy in Africa — but rapid growth has outpaced regulatory frameworks. A sandbox cohort passing through now and receiving licences will likely accelerate formal infrastructure investments, including the physical data facility build-outs that the localisation mandate has also triggered.

🛡️ Lebanon · Security — Update

Update — President Aoun formally backs parliament’s UNIFIL extension petition; Germany’s EU proposal gains Lebanese endorsement as December vacuum plan takes shape

Lebanese President Joseph Aoun has formally welcomed a parliamentary petition calling for an extension of UNIFIL’s mandate beyond December 31, stating that his preferred option is to keep the UN force in place, with Germany’s proposed EU successor mission as a credible second option if extension proves impossible. Aoun’s public endorsement is significant because it gives the German proposal — which until now was an external offer lacking formal Beirut backing — the Lebanese political legitimacy it needed to be treated seriously in UN Security Council deliberations. UNIFIL’s own August 5 data documents more than 1,700 instances of Israeli airspace violations in south Lebanon between June 21 and August 11, plus 100+ airstrikes and 1,800+ projectiles fired — a record that underscores why Aoun believes a security umbrella is existentially necessary. The UN Security Council is expected to begin formal post-UNIFIL discussions in September; Israel has not yet publicly responded to the EU peacekeeping proposal.

Times of Israel · Al Jazeera · Atlantic Council · UNIFIL (August 5 report)
Why it matters to you

Aoun’s endorsement is the missing piece the German proposal needed: a Lebanon-external EU successor force now has both the proposing country (Germany) and the receiving country (Lebanon) aligned. If France and Italy follow — both have major UNIFIL troop commitments — this becomes a viable plan rather than a wish, materially reducing the December security-vacuum tail risk that threatens Beirut real estate values and reconstruction contracts.

⚖️ Lebanon · Banking Reform

Lebanon parliament finance committee advances FSDR banking law with 11 revised articles — IMF watching closely as reform remains precondition for financial assistance

Lebanon’s parliament finance and budget committee has approved 11 new and revised articles to the Financial Stabilization and Depositors Recovery (FSDR) law, addressing provisions that were struck down by the Constitutional Council last October. The revised law — which addresses an estimated $80–83 billion gap between bank deposit liabilities and actual bank assets — is the single most consequential piece of legislation Lebanon can pass this year: the IMF has conditioned any formal Extended Fund Facility (a $3+ billion package stalled since a 2022 staff-level agreement) on un-diluted passage of meaningful banking restructuring. IMF teams have made clear that further dilution of the law’s core provisions would collapse the reform track. Parliamentary dynamics remain contested: some factions are pushing amendments that would weaken bank accountability for deposit losses, and the committee’s 11-article revision must still survive the full plenary vote. If it passes cleanly, Lebanon’s path to IMF funds becomes structurally real for the first time since 2022.

The New Arab · Enterprise AM · Middle East Transparent · IMF (February 2026 visit statement)
Why it matters to you

A clean FSDR passage would ease the macroeconomic pressure on Lebanese clients by opening a path to IMF liquidity and banking sector stabilisation — which over time could reduce the all-cash constraint on larger system procurement projects. A diluted or collapsed law means Bank Audi’s 0% GDP forecast is too optimistic: the real number gets worse.
