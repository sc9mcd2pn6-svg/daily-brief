📈 World · Finance

‘Speedball Capitalism’ Has Dawned — Economist Warns US Finance Is Exciting, Powerful and Potentially Lethal

The Economist’s Schumpeter column sounds a striking alarm: America is experiencing its biggest-ever IPO, its biggest-ever private-funding round and its biggest-ever private-debt deal — all at once. Goldman Sachs forecasts US IPO proceeds will quadruple to a record $160 billion in 2026. The column likens the frenzy to a “speedball” — the heroin-cocaine cocktail famous for killing rock stars — arguing the combination of giga-IPOs, exploding private debt markets and near-zero transparency creates systemic risks that are hard to price.

Beneath the boom, a structural problem: the number of publicly listed US companies has fallen from ~7,000 in 2000 to ~4,000 today, even as the economy has doubled in size. New markets are being created out of thin air; private credit is flowing to borrowers that banks no longer touch. The Economist asks whether regulators — designed for an earlier era of finance — can see the risks building in the dark.

FlyDubai Cockpit Attack: UAE Leads Investigation as Motive and Weapon Source Remain Unknown

The UAE has taken formal lead of the investigation into Wednesday’s bloody in-flight stabbing on FlyDubai Flight 1073 from Dubai to Tel Aviv. The attacker has been identified as an Omani citizen; Israeli Prime Minister Netanyahu stated he had undergone “Islamist radical indoctrination,” though he offered no specifics. The pilot, Indian national Smit Machchhar, is now in good health and is being widely hailed as a hero, alongside the Israeli passengers who subdued the assailant.

Critical questions remain: how the weapon reached the cockpit and whether the attacker acted alone. Israeli officials say they were not informed of the investigation’s full details. The case is reigniting debate about cockpit security protocols on Gulf carriers and direct Israel-route flights, which carry heightened risk profiles in the current regional climate.

🚢 World · Iran / Energy

Iran’s Oil Exports Collapse to ~130K bpd in October — Less Than 6% of Pre-War Levels

The Economist’s figure of the day is stark: Iran exported only 200,000 barrels per day in September — less than one-tenth of its pre-war level. October data is even worse: Energy Intelligence estimates sailings at ~130,000 bpd, with no near-term recovery in sight. The collapse reflects both the US naval blockade of Iranian crude since July and the destruction of Iran’s refinery infrastructure during the conflict. Tehran’s remaining tankers sail to India, China and the Middle East, avoiding all Western eyes.

The supply shock is keeping Brent elevated at $91.93 this morning, up 0.34% on overnight session. For context, Gulf producers (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Kuwait) have largely backfilled the lost volumes through alternative routes, which is why global crude flows have not collapsed — but Iran’s own fiscal revenues are essentially destroyed.

🏗️ World · China / Economy

China’s $5 Trillion ‘Six Networks’ Drive — The Biggest Infrastructure Push Since the Belt & Road

The Economist features China’s new “Six Networks” initiative as a potential turning point for the world economy. The programme — the first time Beijing has bundled its largest infrastructure priorities into one integrated national plan — covers water networks, new-type power grids, computing-power networks, next-generation communications, urban underground pipelines and logistics networks. NDRC head Zheng Shanjie says investment in the Six Networks will exceed 7 trillion yuan (~$1 trillion) in 2026 alone; China Securities estimates total programme cost at more than 26 trillion yuan (~$3.6 trillion) by 2030.

The computing-power network component — hyperscale AI data centres strung across the country — is arguably the most consequential for global tech supply chains. The Economist says this could “help rebalance the world economy,” boosting Chinese domestic demand and potentially cushioning the US-China trade war’s drag on global growth.

💹 World · Rates / Housing

US 10-Year Treasury Holds Near 5.2%; Mortgage Rates Hit 7.28% — Highest Since 2007

The US 30-year fixed mortgage rate has surged to 7.28%, the highest level since 2007, as the 10-year Treasury yield holds just below 5.2% — near a two-decade high. The Federal Reserve is broadly expected to raise rates again in October (market odds ~70%). The proximate drivers are US fiscal concerns (national debt now above $40 trillion), war-elevated energy prices and still-sticky inflation. The NYT reports the average bond yield hit levels not seen since 2002.

Knock-on effects: fewer existing homes are changing hands as sellers stay locked in at lower rates; home builders are squeezed; and any dollar-denominated credit for real estate development is significantly more expensive globally. The Dow fell 0.73% and S&P 0.25% on Thursday, with financials leading the decline.

🇫🇷 France
🏭 France · Politics

Lecornu Survives No-Confidence Motions; Forces 2026 Budget Through Parliament — Ending Months of Deadlock

French Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu has survived the latest in a series of no-confidence motions, with lawmakers from the hard-left LFI and far-right RN both failing to gather enough votes to topple the government. Lecornu used article 49.3 of the constitution for the third and final time to force the 2026 budget through the National Assembly without a vote, concluding months of fraught negotiations. The budget takes effect immediately.

France’s national debt stands at 119% of GDP, and the budget aims to trim the deficit to below 5% through a combination of spending cuts and selective revenue measures. The September 30 public sector strike — 440 arrested — was a direct response to these cuts. With the budget now law, Lecornu’s short-term survival is secured, but the political truce with the RN is fragile and could fracture on the next major flashpoint.

Manchester City Found Guilty of 114 Premier League Charges — Faces Potential 60-Point Deduction or Expulsion

An independent commission has found Manchester City guilty of 114 out of 115 Premier League financial rule violations, covering alleged breaches dating from 2009 to 2018. The Economist devotes a full analysis to the verdict, calling it “the biggest crisis in English football since the 1990s.” Possible sanctions include fines, a points deduction — reports suggest up to 60 points — suspension from the league, or outright expulsion. A separate panel will now decide on punishment; City is expected to appeal.

The charges centre on alleged underpayment of taxes, undisclosed payments to agents and the misleading of Premier League and UEFA financial investigations. The verdict will define the rules of money in elite football for years: if the penalty is heavy, it signals that financial fair play has teeth; if light, it will confirm what critics have long suspected — that the very richest clubs can buy their way through any rule.

Economist: ‘Why House Prices May Be in Trouble’ — 7.28% US Mortgages + 5.2% Yields Compress Affordability Globally

One of The Economist’s most-read pieces this week asks whether global house prices are heading for a sustained correction. The argument: US 30-year mortgage rates at 7.28% and 10-year Treasuries near 5.2% — both at two-decade highs — are no longer a US-only phenomenon. Global bond markets have repriced alongside US yields, meaning mortgage rates in the UK, France and Australia are also elevated. Real (inflation-adjusted) house prices are already falling in several major markets, and transaction volumes are collapsing as buyers are locked out and sellers refuse to accept lower nominal prices.

The Economist notes Lebanon and other high-inflation emerging markets are somewhat insulated by the fact that prime properties trade in dollars with cash buyers — but the pool of such buyers is finite, and if US risk-free rates stay above 5%, dollar cash will increasingly stay at home.

President Aoun Refers 2026 Draft Budget to Parliament On Time — A Rare Signal of Fiscal Discipline

President Joseph Aoun signed Decree No. 1478 on October 2, formally referring Lebanon’s 2026 draft state budget to parliament for deliberation — within the constitutional deadline, a feat Lebanon has rarely managed. The government submitted the budget on time for the first time in years, with Finance Minister Jaber saying it “aligns with reform commitments.” Simultaneously, Aoun convened parliament for an extraordinary session running until October 19, with banking reform legislation and the budget at the top of the agenda. The $5.65 billion budget allocates roughly half to public sector salaries (covering ~300,000 active and retired employees), with 11% earmarked for investment.

L’Orient Today notes the submission is a necessary, if not sufficient, signal to the IMF that Lebanon can manage its fiscal calendar. The staff-level agreement — which slipped from the September Washington meetings — is now being targeted for end-of-year. Parliamentary approval of the budget before January, combined with passage of the banking resolution law, would materially improve the odds of an IMF deal in early 2027.
