War & Intelligence

Leaked Rosstat data reveals 400,000–450,000 Russian war dead — hackers breach Russia’s statistics agency

Two hacker groups, Cyber Partisans and Silent Crow, claim to have breached Rosstat, Russia’s state statistics agency, taking 23 TB of data including millions of death certificates. Their estimates of Russian military fatalities since 2022 range from about 394,000 to 450,000 — far exceeding Moscow’s public acknowledgements. Rosstat confirmed cyberattacks between October 29 and November 1 but denied any data leak. The Economist, which led with an analysis of the breach, found the figures credible and consistent with its own earlier estimates derived from regional budget data.

German security researcher Janis Kluge, who studies Russian war statistics, described the records as “authentic and very rich.” Bloomberg reported that sources familiar with US intelligence placed the figure near 400,000 as realistic. The numbers likely undercount, since roughly six months pass between a death and its civil registration. Russia’s losses now appear to exceed all its post-WWII conflicts combined.

Source: The Economist (14 Nov); Novaya Gazeta Europe (9 Nov); Kyiv Independent; Cybernews

AI & Tech

Jensen Huang resigns as Nvidia CEO — Wall Street loses its AI dealmaker; Dow gains, Nasdaq falls 0.9%

Nvidia founder Jensen Huang resigned as chief executive on Thursday following the board’s independent investigation into his relationship with Alice Huang, a woman US intelligence agencies have identified as a Chinese asset. The board concluded its probe and accepted his departure; a successor has not been named. Huang had been Nvidia’s only CEO in its 33-year history and the driving force behind its $3 trillion market cap. His exit ends his role as the central financier of the AI ecosystem — Nvidia held stakes in 229 private companies including OpenAI and Anthropic worth $48 billion.

Wall Street’s reaction was split: the Dow gained (non-tech sectors relief-rallied) while the Nasdaq fell 0.9% and Nvidia shares continued declining. Silicon Valley start-ups that relied on Huang personally to champion their fundraising are now scrambling. Trump publicly floated Tim Cook’s name as a potential replacement, to widespread scepticism in the industry.

Source: NYT (14 Nov); The Economist (13 Nov); TheStreet (13 Nov); Washington Post

Why it matters to you

Huang was the single person most responsible for steering hyperscaler spending toward the data-centre builds — including the Lagos pipeline where Quasar Nigeria operates. His departure introduces uncertainty over Nvidia’s vendor relationships and AI infrastructure spending pace globally. Watch Anthropic’s IPO pricing this week as the clearest market signal on whether AI-capex confidence holds.

Technology

Microsoft 2-day global outage — email, Teams, Azure and cloud services disrupted; millions without access

A major outage that began Thursday evening knocked out Microsoft’s email, Teams messaging, SharePoint and Azure cloud services for a second consecutive day on Friday. The company described “encouraging” progress on restoration but could not confirm a full fix timeline. The disruption left businesses across North America, Europe and the Middle East without core communications tools for over 36 hours. The NYT noted the outage is “the most recent reminder that the world’s technological underpinnings rely on just a few companies.”

US officials confirmed the outage was unrelated to the FAA ground stop earlier this week, and there is no evidence of a cyberattack. Microsoft is under heightened scrutiny following a 2026 ACCC antitrust ruling in Australia and multiple government inquiries in Europe into cloud concentration. The pattern of repeated outages is driving renewed debate about mandated redundancy in critical infrastructure.

Source: NYT (14 Nov); Computerworld; Bleeping Computer; IT Pro

Why it matters to you

Quasar and A&C use Microsoft 365 for email and Teams for client coordination. A multi-day outage of this nature during a project-critical week (invoices, site commissioning sign-offs) could cost real money. Time to review contingency protocols and consider whether Microsoft’s reliability record warrants a secondary communication fallback.

Finance & AI

Update: Anthropic sets IPO terms — $61 billion offering, $1.2 trillion cap, ANT Nasdaq; pricing this week

Anthropic PBC has set terms for its Nasdaq IPO with a base offering of approximately $61 billion, which would make it the second-largest IPO in history. At the top of the range with the greenshoe exercised, Anthropic could raise up to $74 billion, or $100 billion including a concurrent private placement. The planned ticker is ANT, with retail allocations through Schwab, Fidelity and Robinhood. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley are lead underwriters. Pricing is expected this week with trading possibly starting before Thanksgiving.

The offering values Anthropic at approximately $1.2 trillion, up from a $965 billion valuation at its June 2026 funding round. The IPO will be watched as a critical test of AI-sector sentiment: markets must absorb the offering at the same moment they digest Nvidia’s leadership vacuum, a Microsoft outage and rising US interest rate expectations.

Source: NYT (14 Nov); Axios (26 Oct); Renaissance Capital (10 Nov); NPR

Europe

‘Generational clash’ fuels weekend protests across France, Spain and Germany — pension spending crowding out everything else

Demonstrators are taking to the streets this weekend in Paris, Madrid and several German cities over what the NYT describes as a “generational clash”: governments are spending an ever-larger share of budgets on pensions as retirees live longer, leaving less for housing, healthcare and education. In France, the protests overlap with the LFI (Mélenchon) coalition momentum from Friday’s Paris rally and feed into the 2027 presidential dynamics where Le Pen (at 35–38% in polls) and Mélenchon are positioning around generational resentment from opposite directions.

The trend is structural, not cyclical. Eurozone governments face a choice between raising retirement ages, raising taxes or cutting services — all politically explosive. With the ECB still in a tightening cycle (US Fed also expected to hike in December), fiscal room is narrow. Analysts note it creates the conditions for a sustained period of protest across the bloc through 2027.

Source: NYT (14 Nov); Reuters; L’Orient Today

Geopolitics

Update: Trump-Putin-Xi APEC trilateral confirmed for Shenzhen Nov 18–19 — Iran war, nuclear agenda

China confirmed the first-ever simultaneous summit between the three presidents on the sidelines of the APEC leaders’ meeting in Shenzhen, happening in three days. Trump says nuclear proliferation and “the war against Iran” are on the agenda; he is not expected to raise Russia’s war in Ukraine or the Nvidia scandal. Moscow’s continued insistence on its 2022 hard-line demands, circulated this week in Abu Dhabi, makes a Ukraine breakthrough at this meeting essentially impossible. The meeting is instead focused on the broader US-Russia-China triangle and limiting nuclear spread as the Iran conflict reshapes Middle East equilibria.

European leaders were not invited. The EU is watching with deep unease: a bilateral deal that excludes Brussels on Iran, Taiwan or even a notional Ukraine framework would crystallise the bloc’s strategic irrelevance. The Strait of Hormuz remains the most live flashpoint — The Economist ran a firsthand account this week from forward Iranian positions on the strait, underscoring the conflict has no near-term end in sight.

Source: NYT (14 Nov); China Global South; EWN; The Economist (14 Nov)

Economy & Reform

Post-IMF mission: “more work needed” on banking reform — Financial Gap Law the last hurdle, year-end deadline unclear

The IMF mission that ran November 11–13 in Beirut ended without a preliminary agreement, with the Fund stating publicly that “more work is needed on Lebanon’s banking sector restructuring strategy.” The sticking point remains the Financial Gap Law: parliament has not voted on it, and the central bank has been seeking carve-outs that the IMF is unwilling to endorse. A small committee was formed to try to finalise the text by year-end. France welcomed what it called the “direction of travel,” but analysts note the Banking Restructuring Law passed in August is also under challenge at the Constitutional Council.

Lebanon’s economy is on track to contract 6.4% in 2026 (PM Salam’s own figure, per L’Orient Today). With UNIFIL’s mandate expiring December 31 and the security picture in the south still unresolved, the government faces a convergence of economic, financial and security deadlines in the next six weeks that is without parallel since 2020.

Source: Arab News (14 Nov); IMF statement; L’Orient Today; LBCI; Middle East Online

Art & Restitution

Jewish heirs challenge $180 million van Gogh sale at Sotheby’s — “Blossoming Chestnut Trees” sold under Nazi-era duress

The heirs of a Jewish family are contesting Sotheby’s planned sale of Vincent van Gogh’s Blossoming Chestnut Trees, which is expected to fetch around $180 million at the November sale. The family says the painting was sold under duress during the Nazi occupation — a form of coerced transfer that restitution law in multiple jurisdictions now treats as an unlawful taking. Sotheby’s says it is “committed to working toward a mutually satisfactory resolution” but has not halted the sale.

The case lands during one of the most active restitution periods since the Washington Principles on Nazi-looted art were signed in 1998. Museums and auction houses across Europe and the US are under heightened pressure from heirs emboldened by recent successful claims. A ruling or settlement in this case would set a significant precedent for the secondary market in pre-war European masterworks.

Source: NYT (14 Nov); Sotheby’s; Art Newspaper (Oct 2026)
