US Midterms

Update: Election Day — 50 million Americans already voted; Senate outcome in doubt as Alaska descends into chaos

Today is the midterm. Some 50 million Americans cast early ballots — a surge driven in part by higher Democratic registration rates in Senate battlegrounds including Texas, Kansas, Michigan, Maine and Ohio. The NYT’s final polls put Democrats ahead or tied in seven key Senate races, but the Economist leads its Monday edition with a warning: a disaster in Alaska could deny Democrats the chamber. North Korean hackers — the same Lazarus Group behind the $6.3 billion Binance hack — have targeted Alaska’s election infrastructure, and the state’s ranked-choice voting system means results may take weeks. Michigan also had a late-breaking drama when the loser of the Democratic primary endorsed the Republican contender, but the Economist judges Abdul El-Sayed still likely to win there.

The House appears safely headed to Democrats on a nine-point national lead. Senate control could produce a 50–50 split requiring VP Vance to cast tie-breaking votes, or a narrow Democratic majority. President Trump today described the election as “the most important midterm in our lifetime” while signalling he is prepared for defeat. A Democratic House would end two years of unchecked Republican control, constraining Trump’s tariff and regulatory agenda.

Iran / Energy

Iran signals Strait of Hormuz may reopen within 48 hours — oil prices fall sharply

Iran’s foreign ministry said Monday it is prepared to reopen the Strait of Hormuz “in as little as two days,” citing progress in talks mediated by Qatar. The announcement sent global oil prices sharply lower. Iran’s conditions remain demanding — US sanctions relief and release of billions in frozen Iranian assets — making a formal deal difficult for Washington to accept quickly. More than 35 ships were attacked in the strait last month, the highest toll since the conflict began. Earlier reporting indicates Iran and Oman have been working on a temporary joint maritime corridor as a fallback.

The timing — the eve of US midterm elections — is unlikely to be coincidental. A Hormuz reopening would ease oil prices at a moment when they have been a major consumer grievance driving Democratic gains in the polls. Even a partial opening would reduce the acute pressure on tanker routes and insurance premiums that have been crimping global trade flows since the crisis began.

Source: NYT Evening (2 Nov); Khaleej Times; Crypto Briefing

Why it matters to you

Diesel and heavy fuel oil are the dominant cost for generator-dependent Lebanon and for industrial clients across your portfolio. A sustained Hormuz reopening — even partial — would take pressure off the fuel-oil premiums Lebanese importers have been paying. Watch for confirmation in 24–48 hours before adjusting any dollar-denominated fuel contracts.

China / Trade

China extends export-control playbook beyond rare earths — solar, drones and pharma APIs next

The NYT reports that Beijing is making it structurally harder for other countries to shed their dependence on Chinese manufacturing — and the list of leverage points has grown well beyond the rare-earth metals that first attracted attention. China now controls critical nodes in solar-panel supply chains, drone components (including motors and guidance systems) and the active pharmaceutical ingredients that underpin most of the world’s generic-drug production. New export-licensing requirements are being applied selectively, echoing the rare-earths playbook of years past but at broader scale.

The pattern matters for the tech-infrastructure world: advanced drone components and security electronics — already subject to US ITAR controls — are increasingly also subject to Chinese exit restrictions. Countries and buyers who depend on Chinese-assembled hardware for fire safety, surveillance or BMS equipment face potential dual-sided supply risk.

Source: NYT (2 Nov)

Why it matters to you

Quasar and A&C source a meaningful share of security cameras, control panels and sensor components from Chinese manufacturers. This escalation means dual-sided supply exposure is becoming a real risk — US restrictions on one side, Chinese export controls on the other — especially for any component with dual-use classification.

Act on this: Ask the procurement team for a component-origin audit on the top 20 SKUs across Quasar and A&C — flag any that are Chinese-source and dual-use classified. Consider whether a Turkish or Korean alternative exists for the highest-risk lines.

Lebanon Economy

Lebanon banking reform called “major step” by IMF — but Financial Gap Law still awaits parliament; GDP contraction now 6–12% in 2026

Lebanon’s parliament passed a revised Banking Restructuring Law in August 2026, which the IMF publicly described as a “major step,” saying the parliament version stayed close to the Cabinet-backed text. However, the critical Financial Gap Law — which would allocate the estimated $72 billion in banking losses between shareholders, depositors and the state — remains stuck without a scheduled parliament vote. The IMF has made it clear it will not finalise any program without that law. Meanwhile, the World Bank projects a 6.4% GDP contraction in 2026, while the IIF puts the figure closer to 12%, citing ongoing war-related disruption and fiscal drift.

An IMF mission visited Beirut in September and pressed for a VAT increase (from 11% to 12%, still not implemented), tighter expenditure control and faster bank resolution. Lebanon’s Eurobond prices have been holding at elevated levels relative to a year ago, suggesting some market optimism around eventual restructuring — but that optimism now has a clear near-term test: whether parliament schedules the Financial Gap vote before year-end.

Source: BLOM Invest Bank (Aug–Sep 2026); World Bank (Aug 2026); IMF; EnterpriseAM; L’Orient Today

Why it matters to you

If the Financial Gap Law slips past year-end, the IMF program — and with it the banking-sector stabilisation that makes dollar collections reliable — gets pushed into 2027. For FGC and Quasar, that means another year of navigating USD collections outside the banking system, with all the cashflow risk that implies.
