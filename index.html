💥 World · Iran War

Update: US Launches Second Wave of Strikes on Iran; Tehran Immediately Counter-Attacks

For the second time in three days, US forces struck military targets inside Iran on Tuesday — hitting IRGC air-defence sites, radar systems, maritime assets, mine-laying capabilities, and communications facilities along Iran’s southern shores. The strikes came in retaliation for Iranian tanker attacks in the Strait of Hormuz on Monday and attempted attacks on US military personnel. Oil jumped roughly 4 percent, settling near $91.52/bbl. President Trump threatened to hit Iran “much harder” if it retaliated — and within hours, Iran’s IRGC announced a “regretful” counter-attack. Iranian President Pezeshkian separately offered to return to the June Islamabad Memorandum framework if the US does the same.

US Treasury Secretary Bessent expressed optimism that the EU, Britain, and the UAE are prepared to join the US effort to strangle Iran’s economy through expanded sanctions. The Hormuz flashpoint now triggers simultaneous military, energy, and sanctions escalations: the strait handles roughly 20% of global oil trade, and any prolonged closure would push Brent well above $100. Western officials are bracing for a protracted hot phase with no clear off-ramp in sight.

📉 World · Global Finance

Global Bond Sell-Off Deepens: US 10-Year Hits 4.8%, Japan 3% for First Time Since 1996

A global sell-off in government bonds intensified sharply on Tuesday as the Middle East escalation pushed oil prices up and reignited inflation fears. The yield on the benchmark US 10-year Treasury note briefly hit 4.8% — its highest level in more than a year — while the 30-year reached 5.27%. In Japan, the 10-year bond yield rose above 3% for the first time since 1996. French 10-year yields hit their highest level since 2008. The sell-off reflects a toxic mix: the Iran conflict inflating oil and hence consumer prices, Fed Chair Warsh’s “concerning” inflation comments at Jackson Hole last Friday, and mounting anxiety over sovereign deficits (the US national debt topped $40 trillion in August).

Treasury Secretary Bessent downplayed the rout at a meeting of global finance ministers in Asheville, NC, saying the US is “not in a dire situation.” Markets disagree: rising yields compress valuations across equities, real estate, and EM debt. The Dow fell 0.4% and the S&P 500 followed. For emerging-market borrowers, including Lebanon, the global risk-free rate moving up makes any eventual debt restructuring costlier to price.

🪖 World · US Military

Army Secretary Driscoll Resigns After Clash With Hegseth Over Generals Purge

Secretary of the Army Daniel Driscoll submitted his resignation Monday, effective September 3, after 18 months in post — citing blocked modernisation initiatives and the chaos created by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s firing of senior generals, including Army Chief of Staff Gen. Randy George. According to reporting by Axios and Military Times, the two men had an “icy” relationship: Hegseth was reportedly “suspicious and paranoid” about Driscoll’s close ties to Vice President JD Vance. Driscoll’s departure means the US Army now lacks a confirmed secretary for the first time during an active major combat operation.

The departure is the most senior civilian military resignation since the Iran war began and signals a deepening dysfunction at the top of the Pentagon. With US forces conducting repeated strikes on Iran and managing multiple theatre commitments, analysts at WBUR and elsewhere warn the command vacuum creates operational risk. The NYT reported this morning alongside a broader Trump cabinet reshuffle story.

🇩🇪 World · Europe · Russia

Germany Raises Threat Level to “High,” Closes Russian Consulate After Leipzig Airport Drone Attack

Germany raised its national security threat assessment to “high” on Tuesday — the second-highest level — and ordered the closure of Russia’s consulate in Bonn and the Russian Cultural House in Berlin, while tightening entry controls for Russian nationals. The actions followed Berlin formally attributing last month’s discovery of an explosive-laden drone near a Ukrainian cargo plane at Leipzig/Halle Airport to Russian intelligence services. The German government said the drone was intended to destroy the aircraft and trigger a wider incident, potentially targeting the air-freight corridor used to supply Ukraine.

The move marks Germany’s sharpest direct response to Russian covert operations on its soil since the 2022 invasion and is likely to accelerate EU-wide expulsions of Russian diplomatic staff. For European businesses, the tightening travel and commercial environment between Russia and the EU — already near-complete — signals that any residual commercial ties to Russia carry growing legal and reputational risk.

✈️ World · China Military

Economist: Chinese Jets Stage Mock Air Combat Over Egypt — PLA Goes Global

This week’s Economist reveals that Chinese military aircraft have been conducting simulated dogfights with Egyptian air-force jets over Egyptian airspace — a striking new chapter in China’s “dogfight diplomacy.” The exercises, part of a broader pattern of joint military activities across Africa and the Middle East, show the People’s Liberation Army Air Force projecting power thousands of kilometres beyond China’s traditional sphere. Egypt is also a major Chinese Belt-and-Road partner and the gateway to Red Sea commerce, giving the exercises both military and strategic-trade significance.

The Economist frames this as part of Beijing’s methodical expansion of military reach under the cover of joint exercises — similar to how the US projected power globally in the Cold War through basing rights and joint drills. For the Middle East and Africa, the implication is that China is increasingly capable of projecting force, not just economic influence, into those regions — complicating the security calculations of regional governments and their Western partners.

Economist: Miami’s Extraordinary Economic Rise — 17 Homes Sold Above $50m Last Year

The Economist this week examines what it calls the “extraordinary transformation” of Miami’s economy over the past five years: a combination of low taxes, warm weather, and business-friendly policies has drawn in tech and financial firms plus very wealthy individuals in numbers that have reshaped the city. Last year, 17 Miami homes sold for more than $50 million — nearly half the entire US national total at that price point. The city now hosts a concentration of hedge funds, private equity shops, and crypto firms that rivals New York neighbourhoods. The Economist asks whether the growth is sustainable or a bubble driven by optionality and tax arbitrage.

The Miami case is directly relevant as a global benchmark: the same playbook — low regulation, fresh capital, diaspora inflows — is visible in prime Beirut at a much earlier stage. If diaspora buyers who moved to Miami in 2021–23 now rotate some of their appreciation into Lebanese real estate, Beirut could see a second leg of demand. Equally, Miami’s data-centre boom (driven by the same financial-sector inflow) offers a parallel for Lagos as financial firms anchor digital infrastructure demand.

🇱🇧 Lebanon
🕊️ Lebanon · Security · Diplomacy

Aoun Travels to Rome for UNIFIL Succession Talks; France-Italy Replacement Force Takes Shape

Lebanese President Joseph Aoun is travelling to Rome this week for direct talks with Italian counterparts on the future of UNIFIL — the UN peacekeeping force in south Lebanon whose mandate expires in December. A joint French-Italian initiative to establish a replacement or reformed force is now taking concrete shape, with both Paris and Rome having offered contributing troops. The discussions focus on whether a new body could command broader Lebanese sovereignty, given Hezbollah secretary-general Na’im Qassem’s outright rejection of the current framework agreement, which he calls “illegitimate.” The US has signalled the next round of Rome talks (the 9th round) will happen in September.

The December deadline is immovable: if UNIFIL is not renewed or replaced by then, south Lebanon reverts to an unmonitored border zone that would likely reignite hostilities. L’Orient Today (Tier 3 independent) notes that Hezbollah’s refusal to disarm is the central sticking point — and the Israelis have made clear (Defence Minister Katz again this week) they will not withdraw from their remaining positions in southern Lebanon until Hezbollah is fully disarmed. The window is narrowing rapidly.

📊 Lebanon · Economy

Lebanon Economy: Hormuz Oil Spike Compounds Fuel Crisis; Eurobond Yields Hold at 61%

Brent crude hitting $91.52 — the highest level in weeks — is a direct blow to Lebanon, which imports almost all its fuel and has limited storage capacity. The Hormuz disruption caused by the Iran-US conflict has already reduced Gulf fuel flows into the Eastern Mediterranean, pushing up local diesel and generator-fuel prices at a time when the public electricity grid remains largely non-functional. The World Bank’s projection of a 6.4% GDP contraction for Lebanon in 2026 now looks optimistic given an oil price that was modelled at lower levels. The IMF’s September Beirut visit — to assess reform progress — comes in this context of compounding shocks.

On the capital-markets side, Lebanon’s BLOM Bond Index (BBI) was last marked at 29.69 with a weighted yield of 60.74% as of late August. The global bond sell-off described above means Lebanon’s restructuring talks with creditors now face a higher global risk-free rate floor, making any eventual new-money instrument structurally more expensive. BLOMINVEST notes that Eurobonds dipped slightly last week even as diplomatic headlines (IMF progress, FSDR passage) supported sentiment earlier in the summer.
