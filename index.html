📈 World · Economy

US Owes $40 Trillion — and Washington Shrugs

The US national debt has crossed $40 trillion, a figure the NYT leads with on its Saturday morning edition under the headline “In Trump’s Washington, Ballooning National Debt Stirs Little Action.” The politics of deficit spending have shifted so dramatically that neither party is treating the milestone as a crisis: Republicans are focused on tax cuts and defence, Democrats on defending social spending, and the bond market — once the supposed enforcer of fiscal discipline — has been relatively quiet despite yields remaining elevated.

Analysts warn that the combination of persistent primary deficits and the Fed’s expected tightening cycle creates a dangerous feedback loop: as interest costs eat up a larger share of the federal budget, the government’s room for manoeuvre in any future downturn or crisis shrinks sharply. Rating agencies have flagged the trajectory but declined to act.

🏦 World · Markets

Fed Chair Warsh at Jackson Hole: Rate Hike in Play, “Inflation Has Work to Do”

In his first major speech as Federal Reserve Chair at the Jackson Hole symposium, Kevin Warsh said the central bank has “work to do” if inflation does not move toward the 2% target “clearly and at sufficient speed.” Markets initially welcomed his relatively clear framing — a departure from his earlier opacity — but sold off after investors concluded rate hikes are now back on the table. A majority of futures market participants now price a September hike. The Economist also covered Warsh’s speech prominently.

Warsh declined to commit to a “reaction function” or give forward guidance, instead laying out a governance philosophy: the Fed should not let markets look to it for their “next trade.” Summer inflation data came in better than expected but Warsh said he saw no evidence that underlying trends had meaningfully improved. The S&P 500 fell 0.25% and the Dow shed 9 points on Friday after his remarks.

🛢️ World · Iran War

Update: Six Months of War — US Oil Reserve at 1982 Lows, Iranians “Hungry and Confused”

Exactly six months after the US and Israel launched their attack on Iran, the NYT Evening newsletter takes stock on Friday: the war is dragging on, fuel costs have soared, and the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve has fallen to its lowest level since 1982, with more than 352 million barrels drawn down. The SPR now sits below 300 million barrels for the first time since it was originally filled, and the GAO has flagged that a quarter of remaining inventory may not be available for drawdown due to maintenance backlogs. Brent held at $88.22 Friday.

The human cost inside Iran is sharpening: an artist in Tehran told the NYT “people are hungry, confused and unable to find a way out.” Earlier hopes that US intervention would force political change have faded; the Islamic government appears likely to survive and the focus among ordinary Iranians has shifted to economic survival. The war has become deeply unpopular in the US, in part because of fuel prices.

🎈 World · Ukraine

Ukraine Revives an 18th-Century Weapon: Military Balloons Launching Drones Deep Inside Russia

The Economist’s Friday edition leads its Editor’s Picks with a striking story: Ukraine has been using tethered and free-flying military balloons to carry and launch drones that strike deep within Russian territory, far beyond the range of conventional ground-launched systems. The balloon-drone combination exploits radar blind spots, is cheap to produce, and has reportedly caused disruption at several Russian military and industrial sites. The Economist frames it as a reminder that low-tech ingenuity can offset some disadvantages in a war between unequal powers.

The story arrives as Ukraine’s battlefield position has improved following its recapture of 745 km² in Dnipropetrovsk earlier this week, and as CIA Chief Ratcliffe’s secret Moscow visit highlighted US pressure on Russia to negotiate. The balloon tactic has so far received little Western media coverage, making the Economist piece the first major English-language outlet to detail it.

👑 World · Europe

King Harald V of Norway Dies at 89; Son Becomes King Haakon VIII

King Harald V of Norway died peacefully at Oslo University Hospital on the morning of Friday August 28 at the age of 89, making him Europe’s longest-reigning and oldest monarch at the time of his death. His 35-year reign (1991–2026) was marked by a deliberate modernisation of the Norwegian monarchy: he opened the institution to marriages with so-called “commoners”, including his own wife Queen Sonja, and steadily expanded its role as a symbol of national unity rather than political power. As a child Harald fled with his family to the United States during the Nazi occupation, spending the war years partly in residence at the White House.

His only son, Crown Prince Haakon, immediately acceded as King Haakon VIII. Norway entered a formal period of mourning. The NYT carried an obituary noting Harald’s 35-year record and his role as a diplomat of Scandinavian soft power.

Update: Iceland Votes Today on EU Accession Restart — No Camp Held Narrow Lead Before Polls Opened

Iceland’s long-anticipated consultative referendum on whether to reopen EU membership negotiations is taking place today, Saturday August 29. Polls closed last night with the No camp holding a slender lead — a final Gallup poll showed 51.6% against and 48.4% in favour — but the margin is well within the uncertainty band and turnout patterns could swing the outcome. The vote does not decide membership itself: a Yes instructs the government to begin accession talks, which could take a decade or more to conclude. A No would likely shelve the question until after the next election.

Key drivers of Yes sentiment include Trump’s Arctic territorial posturing and the security lessons of Russia’s war in Ukraine; key drivers of No sentiment include Iceland’s fishing rights and cultural sovereignty concerns. The result is expected to be known by Saturday evening Beirut time.

Nigeria Mandates Onshore Data Storage by Jan 2027 — 4,700 Cyberattacks per Week, Highest in Africa

The Central Bank of Nigeria has issued a circular requiring all financial institutions to ensure that payment transaction data generated within Nigeria is stored and managed within Nigeria by January 1, 2027. The mandate is part of a broader “digital sovereignty” push that mirrors similar moves in India and Indonesia. Separately, cybersecurity firm Check Point reports that Nigerian organisations faced an average of 4,700 cyberattacks per week in the first eight months of 2026 — the highest weekly rate recorded across Africa — with financial services and government agencies the prime targets. Angola has surpassed Nigeria in absolute incident count only because Nigerian defences have improved.

Nigeria now has 21 operational data centres with five more high-capacity facilities nearing completion on the Lagos-Lekki corridor. The data-sovereignty mandate will accelerate onshore infrastructure investment, as foreign-hosted payment data must be migrated, but it will also raise compliance costs and complexity for operators whose systems span multiple jurisdictions.

💻 France · Cybersecurity

France Tax Authority Hacked: Data of 678,000 Taxpayers Stolen in Summer Cyberattack

Hackers stole the personal tax data of approximately 678,000 French individuals and businesses in a breach of the Directorate General of Public Finances (DGFiP) that occurred in late June 2026 but was only publicly disclosed on August 12 when a cybercriminal operating as “ZeroBytes” advertised the stolen files on a cybercrime forum. France’s Public Accounts Minister David Amiel confirmed the breach and announced that all affected citizens would be contacted directly from August 17. The data included income, fiscal reference numbers, and in some cases business registration details.

The incident is one of the largest cyberattacks on a French government institution in recent years. Authorities are investigating; no group has yet been officially attributed. The breach follows a March 2026 attack on French health records that exposed data on 15 million patients, underlining what cybersecurity experts describe as a systemic vulnerability in French government IT infrastructure.

Update: Lebanon’s Bank Resolution Law Passes, But L’Orient Today Finds Key Issues Unresolved

Parliament adopted its amended Financial Sector Development and Recovery (FSDR) law on August 12, addressing some of the IMF’s core demands on bank resolution — but L’Orient Today’s analysis finds that the compromise legislation leaves three significant gaps: ambiguous division of powers between the Banque du Liban and the newly established resolution authority; unresolved questions about shareholder participation in bank recapitalization; and overlapping provisions that legal experts say will cause confusion and litigation during implementation. The IMF had formally welcomed the law as a “major step” but explicitly warned that “implementation risks remain.”

Finance Minister Yassine Jaber has estimated combined direct and indirect losses from Lebanon’s successive conflicts since 2023 at up to $20 billion. With Lebanon’s GDP now projected to contract 6.4% in 2026 (World Bank) and inflation rising to 17.5%, the IMF’s September return to Beirut — confirmed for next month — will hinge on whether the FSDR implementation plan satisfies the fund’s technical team despite the legislative gaps.

Clients in Beirut who are already squeezed on payments could face additional stress. This is the critical variable for Lebanon’s economic trajectory over the next six months.
