Update: Brazil presidential runoff — Bolsonaro narrowly ahead in final polls as votes were counted overnight

US Economy

Trumponomics two years on: poor and middle households bear the cost — Economist’s verdict

The Economist’s lead analysis publishes its two-year gauge of Trumponomics, combining the estimated impact of tariffs, higher petrol prices and the One Big Beautiful Bill Act on American households by income band. The finding is unambiguous: poor and middle-income households have seen purchasing power eroded; rich households have been the net beneficiaries. The tariff burden falls disproportionately on lower earners who spend more of their income on goods, while tax cuts in the OBBBA skewed toward high earners.

The analysis carries a political dimension ahead of Tuesday’s US midterm elections: Democratic polling leads of around 11 points partly reflect this distributional reality. A companion Economist piece cautions, however, that polling errors in recent US elections have consistently underestimated Republican performance — history suggests the final wave, if it materialises, will be smaller than surveys indicate.

Source: The Economist (25 Oct 2026)

India – Economy

India stabilises its rupee — but the Reserve Bank is deploying ever-harsher tools to hold the line

The Economist highlights India’s increasingly interventionist exchange-rate management: the Reserve Bank of India has been using “ever harsher measures” to keep the rupee below 100 to the dollar. While the currency has stabilised on paper, the tools — large dollar sales, forward market intervention and restrictions on speculative flows — are straining foreign-exchange reserves and raising questions about sustainability in a tariff-disrupted, dollar-strong global environment.

A rupee that breaks through 100/USD would signal a significant loss of central bank credibility and could roil Indian markets, which carry growing weight in global equity indices. India’s dilemma — protect competitiveness while managing inflation — is the same one facing most large emerging markets, and its chosen tools are among the most aggressive currently in use.

Source: The Economist (25 Oct 2026)

Nigeria – Data Centres

Lagos data centre race: 146 MW in pipeline, but power remains the defining bottleneck

The Lagos data centre market has accelerated significantly: Nxtra by Airtel is building a $120M hyperscale facility at Eko Atlantic aimed at AI workloads; Equinix has opened its LG3 facility ($22M, Victoria Island); and Lagos State government has confirmed 146 MW of additional grid capacity in planning. Africa Data Centres, Kasi Cloud (scalable to 100 MW at Lekki), Digital Parks Africa’s carrier-neutral NDC1 and Open Access DC ($240M planned) are all simultaneously active. Nigeria’s total data centre capacity is projected to jump from 56 MW (2025) to 218 MW by 2030.

The defining constraint is power: Africa’s average power usage effectiveness (PUE) runs at 1.67 vs a global benchmark of 1.58, and every major Lagos operator self-generates due to grid instability. The market is growing at 20–25% per year in power demand terms, making generator reliability and fuel logistics — not connectivity — the primary operational challenge for data centre clients in Lagos.

France – Social

T−10 days to France’s general strike: government met unions October 21, no deal reached

The November 5 general strike — France’s largest in decades — is ten days away with no sign of a resolution. Prime Minister Lecornu’s government met the five participating unions (CGT, CFDT, FO, UNSA, FSU) on October 21 but failed to bridge the gap over wages, purchasing power and the 2027 budget dispute. Four rail unions (CGT-Cheminots, UNSA-Ferroviaire, SUD-Rail, CFDT-Cheminots) have jointly confirmed they will halt TGV, Intercités and TER services; Paris RER and Transilien status is still being negotiated.

The strike falls against the backdrop of Moody’s fresh warning on French sovereign debt and spreading social unrest that has already rattled French credit markets. With a government unable to command parliamentary support and no credible budget deal in sight, the November 5 action is the most significant test yet of whether the Lecornu administration can survive through next year’s presidential cycle.
