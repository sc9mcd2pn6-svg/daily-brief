SpaceX Starship Reaches Orbit for the First Time — 26 Starlink V3 Satellites Deployed in Historic Flight 14

SpaceX completed the 14th test flight of its 407-foot Starship rocket early Monday, sending the vehicle into orbit for the first time and successfully deploying 26 next-generation Starlink V3 satellites — each with 10× the broadband capacity of the current generation. The uncrewed craft launched from Starbase in Boca Chica, Texas, orbited Earth roughly six times, then executed a planned deorbit burn for a controlled splashdown off South America approximately ten hours after liftoff. Engineers at the facility cheered as mission milestones were confirmed. The achievement clears the last major engineering threshold before Starship can carry NASA’s Artemis lunar landers and, eventually, SpaceX’s own Mars missions.

The orbital milestone transforms Starship from an ambitious prototype into an operational launch platform. Musk has stated that AI revenue from Starlink will exceed all other SpaceX revenue within months; the V3 constellation will support that bet with dramatically higher throughput per satellite. CNBC and CNN both noted that with Starship now orbital-capable, the economics of satellite internet — and of SpaceX’s planned orbital AI data-centre programme — change fundamentally. Regulatory observers also note this removes a key uncertainty blocking Starship’s use for the US military’s “rapid global delivery” contract.

As AI Accelerates, Governments Are Increasingly Left Behind — Global Policy Vacuum Widens

The NYT’s Monday morning lead argued that the gap between AI model capability and government policymaking has become wider than at any previous point in AI’s history. Frontier models are now advancing quarterly; legislative cycles take years. The report notes that the EU’s AI Act — finalised only this spring — is already being described by technologists as regulating the previous generation of AI, not the current one. The White House has taken the opposite approach from Brussels: deregulatory posture and a private dinner between Trump and Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei signalled that the US will lean on voluntary corporate commitments rather than statute. Neither approach is keeping pace.

The practical consequence is a global patchwork: stringent regulation in the EU, laissez-faire in the US, state-capture of AI in China, and a governance vacuum in much of the developing world. Middle-income countries — including Lebanon and Nigeria — are on the receiving end of AI deployments designed elsewhere, with no domestic frameworks to govern how AI is used in infrastructure, security systems, healthcare or public services. The Economist’s September 28 edition noted that Nayib Bukele is using AI extensively in El Salvador to run public services, with minimal oversight — a cautionary template for the region.

Ireland Is Helping Supply Russia’s War Machine — Economist and OCCRP Name Aughinish Alumina

The Economist’s Monday edition ran a pointed investigation titled “Sanctions, Schmanctions” detailing how the Russian-owned Aughinish Alumina refinery in County Limerick — the largest alumina plant in Europe — continues to export hundreds of thousands of tonnes of alumina to Russian smelters, whose aluminium then feeds sanctioned arms manufacturers. The OCCRP investigative consortium documented the chain in detail: the same aluminium supply route has reached factories producing munitions that struck Mariupol, a Kyiv children’s hospital and residential blocks in western Ukraine. The EU banned Russian aluminium imports in February 2025 but has not restricted alumina exports to Russia, leaving the loophole open.

Ireland’s government has resisted closing the gap: Prime Minister Micheál Martin argued that sanctions “should not damage ourselves more than Russia,” citing 1,000 direct jobs. The EU’s foreign ministers are under pressure — from Latvia in particular — to add Aughinish to the sanctions list, but Ireland has blocked it inside the Council. Euronews and the Irish Times confirm this is now a live political issue ahead of the EU’s 16th sanctions package deliberations. The contradiction — Ireland publicly supports Ukraine while one of its largest industrial facilities arms Ukraine’s enemy — is becoming diplomatically untenable.

Lebanon & IMF May Sign Staff-Level Agreement Today — Finance Minister in Washington for Historic Meeting

Lebanon’s Finance Minister Yassine Jaber flew to Washington on Monday for a direct meeting with IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva, with a source close to the process telling Asharq Al-Awsat and Arab News that a staff-level agreement “could be signed as soon as Monday.” If finalised, it would be Lebanon’s first formal IMF staff-level agreement since the 2019 financial collapse, and would open the path to a full lending programme — potentially unlocking hundreds of millions in emergency financing plus the credibility signal that unblocks bilateral and multilateral donor flows. US News and the Print (India) confirm that the bank restructuring law passed in August was the key milestone that made this meeting possible. The IMF had welcomed that law in August; Jaber’s Washington trip is the follow-through. Lebanon still needs the financial gap law to receive actual disbursements, but the staff-level agreement marks a qualitative shift from years of stalled talks to active programme design.

Ann Temkin Steps Down After 18 Years as MoMA’s Chief Curator — First Woman to Hold the Role

Ann Temkin, the first woman to serve as chief curator of painting and sculpture at the Museum of Modern Art in New York, announced she is stepping down after 18 years in the role. The NYT reports that Temkin is credited with reshaping MoMA’s permanent collection display — including the landmark 2019 rehang that broke down chronological and medium silos — and overseeing major acquisitions in contemporary and postwar art. Her tenure coincided with a period of significant market appreciation in the works MoMA holds, as well as growing institutional pressure over diversifying the canon.

The succession at MoMA is closely watched by the contemporary art market: chief curator decisions about what MoMA acquires, how it displays works, and which artists it champions have direct market consequences. The transition will come at a moment of particular collector and institutional focus on postwar European abstraction — a category directly relevant to the Castellani family of works. No successor has been named.
