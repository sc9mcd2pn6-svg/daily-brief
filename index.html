☠️ World · Iran / Hormuz

Iran Presents UNGA Ceasefire Roadmap — 60-Day Truce, Phased Hormuz Reopening; US Given 4–5 Days to Respond

Iran’s Supreme National Security Council secretary Mohsen Rezaei presented Washington with a formal roadmap at the UN General Assembly: a regionwide ceasefire of up to 60 days, a phased reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, and an end to American blockade measures. The document calls for a simultaneous halt to Iranian attacks on Arab Gulf neighbours and seeks US agreement to a timeline for substantive nuclear negotiations while the truce holds. Rezaei attached a clock: the US has four to five days to respond before the offer lapses.

Oil markets are oscillating — Brent touched $108 intraday before settling near $107 on the Hormuz reopening hope. Trump has ruled out lifting the blockade until Tehran demonstrates “sufficient goodwill” and the White House has not publicly acknowledged the document. After a ceasefire earlier this summer partially collapsed in July, expectations in Washington are deliberately muted. But diplomats see the Iranian move as the most substantive peace signal since hostilities began in February, spurred partly by Iran’s worsening economic pain from the blockade.

📈 World · Finance / Rates

US Bond Yields at 2007 Highs — 10-Year at 5.12%, Mortgages Cross 7%; Biggest Single-Day Jump Since the Tariff Shock

The US 10-year Treasury yield surged 13.7 basis points to 5.12% on Thursday — its highest level since 2007 and the largest single-session jump since the tariff turmoil hit markets in 2025. The 30-year fixed mortgage rate crossed 7.03%, up nearly a full percentage point in seven months and more than four points above its 2021 level. The move was triggered by a combination of stronger-than-expected US private-sector data, the Dow falling for a third straight session, and a market that is now pricing in a Federal Reserve rate hike as its base case.

The Economist’s cover this week (“The new mortgage shock”) frames this as the opening phase of a structural deterioration in global housing markets: previous rate cycles were cushioned by equity buffers built up during Covid; those buffers are now largely spent, and households in the US, UK and Australia face genuine distress. The NYT reports that US existing home sales hit their lowest level in more than a year in August, while bond-market strategists at Charles Schwab warn the “blistering yield rally” is now overshadowing the Trump–Xi talks.

🤟 World · US–China

Update: Trump–Xi Summit Closes — Trade Truce Extended, Two Pandas, No Breakthrough on AI or Taiwan

The two-day Washington summit ended with a lavish state dinner and effusive personal warmth from President Trump — but substantive outcomes were thin. The most concrete result was an extension of the bilateral trade truce (due to expire November 10), preventing an imminent tariff escalation. Trump gave Xi a tour of the White House ballroom, inspired partly by Beijing’s Great Hall of the People; Xi announced China would send two giant pandas to Atlanta Zoo. Tech sector CEOs attended the state dinner alongside both leaders, signalling where the real US–China commercial relationship is being managed.

The Economist warns this week that what the summit “has—and hasn’t—achieved” amounts to managed stability rather than resolution: Xi is playing a long game, seeking predictability during China’s domestic economic stress, while Trump frames personal chemistry as the substitute for structural agreement. Joint communiqué language on critical-mineral export controls and AI safety notification remains under close watch. China’s AI development lags the US in key areas, but Beijing holds structural advantages that the NYT identifies as the real competitive tension beneath the smiling optics.

🚗 World · Middle East / US Strategy

America’s Middle East Hegemony Is Ending — US Withdraws from Iraq Sep 30; Regional Powers Rushing to Fill the Vacuum

The Economist leads its issue this week with a sweeping assessment: American hegemony in the Middle East is definitively ending. The US is withdrawing all remaining forces from Iraq by September 30, 2026, and the Iran war — rather than cementing American dominance — has exposed the vulnerability of US military infrastructure across the Gulf, sparking internal White House debates about cutting the broader regional presence. Trump’s empty threats (including this week’s “annihilate” warning) have drained US deterrence credibility to near-zero.

As the US exits, three regional powers are jostling to fill at least part of the vacuum: Turkey, which has expanded its military and economic footprint from Libya to the Gulf; Saudi Arabia, which is repositioning itself as a neutral broker; and Israel, which is digging in militarily across Lebanon and northern Syria. The Economist’s Middle East team concludes this could usher in either a new era of regional score-settling and violence, or — cautiously — a genuine process of self-determined regional order-building. The chaos window is the near term.

Netanyahu at UNGA: “Wars Against Barbarians” — Dozens Walk Out; Abbas Virtual After US Visa Denial

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu addressed the UN General Assembly on Thursday, defending the wars in Gaza and Iran as fights “against barbarians” and accusing critics of hypocrisy. Dozens of delegates walked out in protest as he took the podium; outside, hundreds protested on the streets of New York. Supporters inside the hall responded with chants of “Am Yisrael Chai.” Palestinian Authority President Mahmoud Abbas addressed the Assembly virtually — the US had denied him a visa — and called Israeli actions in Gaza and the West Bank “a malicious colonial scheme,” calling for a two-state solution.

The contrasting scenes underlined the depth of the diplomatic rupture. The NYT notes that 51 countries have now signed a joint statement calling on Israel to end military operations in Gaza, and the walkout count at Netanyahu’s speech was among the largest in recent UNGA history. The UN Secretary-General separately called the situation in Gaza “unconscionable” and renewed calls for a humanitarian ceasefire that Washington continues to block.

🛰 World · AI / Space

Google One Week from Launching First AI Data Centre Satellite — Refrigerator-Size, Solar-Powered, Answers Queries from Orbit

Google says it is approximately one week from launching a refrigerator-size satellite capable of answering simple AI queries from low-Earth orbit — the first major step toward establishing what would be the world’s first orbital AI data centre. The NYT was given inside access to the programme. The satellite runs on solar power, addressing one of the central anxieties of the ground-based data centre boom: energy demand. Jeff Bezos, Elon Musk and Sam Altman have all pledged support for orbital data-centre concepts for the same reason.

Experts are divided on feasibility at scale: latency from orbit remains a fundamental limitation for most commercial applications, and the economics of launching and replacing hardware are still punishing at current launch costs. But if Google’s prototype works, it would represent a new vector in the AI infrastructure race — and potentially a disruptive one for the ground-based data centre industry that has spent the last three years locking in land, power and cooling capacity on the assumption orbital computing was decades away.

37 Detainees Die in Nigerian Security Corps Custody in Minna — Premium Times Investigation Raises Systemic Concerns

Premium Times (Nigeria’s leading independent investigative outlet) reports that 37 detainees held by the Nigeria Security and Civil Defence Corps (NSCDC) died in Minna, Niger State, on September 17. The NSCDC is a federal paramilitary agency responsible for protection of critical infrastructure including pipelines, power stations and public buildings. Details on the cause of death are under investigation, but the scale — 37 deaths in a single custody incident — is among the worst on record for a Nigerian security institution outside of conflict zones.

The NSCDC has a mandate that overlaps directly with the kinds of facilities — data centres, telecoms infrastructure, industrial sites — where Quasar Nigeria operates. Separately, Premium Times has been shortlisted for the African Investigative Journalism Award 2026 for its investigation into lead-poisoning from battery recycling, underlining its track record on hard-to-report institutional stories. Nigeria’s Tinubu administration has not yet issued a formal statement on the Minna deaths.

🏛 France · Politics

French Senate Elections Sunday — Centre-Right Expected to Hold, but Far-Right RN Poised for Historic Senate Gains

France votes Sunday, September 27 to elect 178 senators across 64 constituencies — roughly half the upper chamber. The electoral college is made up almost entirely of town councillors and local delegates, making this year’s vote structurally different from 2023: the Rassemblement National’s strong showing in this year’s municipal elections has changed the composition of the electoral college in several departments, giving the far-right a structural path to Senate seats for the first time. Connexion France expects the centre-right to retain its overall majority but sees RN gains as highly probable.

The stakes are significant heading into the 2027 presidential cycle: an expanded RN Senate presence would constrain Macron’s remaining room to legislate and further shift the centre of gravity in French politics rightward. The Konrad-Adenauer-Stiftung notes that a Senate with even 30–40 RN members would be a qualitative change in France’s institutional landscape, strengthening Le Pen’s claim to mainstream governance ahead of a third presidential run.

Salam Meets Syria’s New Leadership at UNGA — Lebanon–Syria Rapprochement Accelerates, High-Level Damascus Delegation Due in Beirut

Lebanese Prime Minister Nawaf Salam met Syrian President Ahmad Al Shara in New York on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly, marking a significant step in the reset of Lebanese–Syrian relations following the collapse of the Assad regime in December 2024. L’Orient Today (independent, Beirut) reports that a high-level Syrian delegation of six ministers and senior officials is expected in Beirut imminently to discuss bilateral economic issues. In early September, Lebanese and Syrian security services jointly dismantled a cross-border cell planning attacks inside Syria — the first such operation under the new Syrian government.

The rapprochement is economically significant: Syria has historically been Lebanon’s land bridge for exports to the Arab Gulf, a route closed since 2011. Reopening it would lower Lebanese export costs substantially and reopen markets for Lebanese manufacturers and traders. The Salam government is treating the Syria normalisation as a quiet economic lifeline, running in parallel with the IMF reform track. L’Orient Today notes that the pace of diplomatic contact between the two governments has accelerated sharply since August.

🚨 Lebanon · Security

Update: Hezbollah Drone Attack on Israeli Troops Kills 3 in IDF Strikes; Mais al-Jabal Hospital Set on Fire

Hezbollah launched a drone at Israeli troops in Israeli-held south Lebanon; the IDF responded with strikes that killed at least three people and wounded 23, according to the Times of Israel and Al Jazeera. Separately, L’Orient Today (independent) reports that the Israeli army set fire to a hospital in Mais al-Jabal — the Lebanese Health Ministry has formally condemned the act. The incident is the latest in a pattern of systematic destruction of inhabited and civic structures in occupied southern villages that international human rights monitors have been documenting since June.

The broader framework remains unchanged: Israel insists it will not withdraw from south Lebanon until Hezbollah disarms; Hezbollah and the Salam government both reject this condition as non-negotiable. UNIFIL’s mandate expires December 31, leaving no international monitoring buffer. The Lebanese Army cannot fully deploy pending Israeli withdrawal. With the Iran ceasefire roadmap now on the table (see World section), there is a faint possibility that a broader US–Iran deal could unlock movement on south Lebanon — but diplomats are not counting on it.

Prime Beirut Rental Yields Hit 5–7% in Fresh Dollars — Competitive with Dubai, Cyprus and Athens

Lebanon’s prime real estate market — Achrafieh, Hamra, Verdun and the Metn suburbs — is showing a quiet but real recovery dynamic. Rental yields in prime Beirut neighbourhoods are now running at 5–7% annually in fresh dollars, making Lebanese real estate genuinely competitive with regional markets including Dubai, Cyprus and Athens when measured on a yield basis. All transactions are cash — there is no mortgage market — which eliminates leverage risk but limits the buyer pool to diaspora cash and local fresh-dollar holders.

A RM Real Estate Consultant analysis published in mid-2026 identifies a demand wave driven by diaspora buyers, fresh-dollar liquidity from the partial banking reform, and growing recognition that prime locations are exceptional long-term value at current prices — still 20% below their 2019 peak in prime zones, and 35% below in peripheral areas. The mismatch between seller expectations and buyer patience persists, but deal volumes are rising. The Economist’s cover story on global mortgage shock this week (rising yields, falling affordability) is driving international investors to compare yield markets — which benefits Beirut over cash-strapped Western cities.
