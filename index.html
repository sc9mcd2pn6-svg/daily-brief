🚨 World · Iran / UNGA

Trump at UNGA: “Do I Annihilate the Islamic Republic?” — Deal or Obliteration, He Says, After the Midterms

In a striking address to the 81st UN General Assembly on Tuesday, President Trump told assembled world leaders he faced a binary choice on Iran: negotiate a final deal, or “annihilate the Islamic Republic and do it quickly, never giving them a chance to kill and destroy people and countries again.” He framed the decision as one he would make after the November midterm elections, giving a roughly two-month window. Cuban diplomats walked out mid-speech after Trump declared Cuba “an absolutely failed state.”

On the sidelines, Trump’s envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner held a direct meeting with Iran’s foreign minister, which Trump called “a very good meeting.” Iran’s Revolutionary Guards responded that Washington would gain “nothing but failure” from any attack. Most attending heads of state avoided direct condemnation of the US but several criticised the Iran war and Israeli conduct; outgoing UN Secretary-General António Guterres issued a stark warning about deepening global divisions.

🏭 World · US–China / Diplomacy

Xi Arrives in Washington Today — Rare Earths, AI, Trade and Taiwan on Summit Agenda

Chinese President Xi Jinping arrived in Washington on September 23 for a three-day state visit — the format includes a White House bilateral, a state dinner, and a National Archives tour. The NYT flags that Xi is playing a longer game: facing an American president constrained by war and politics, China’s leader is content to “lock in a tactical truce” while he shores up a fragile domestic economy. The Economist notes that American bosses are pushing back on Trump’s narrative that China is “opening up,” with executives on the ground still facing mounting barriers.

Key agenda items are rare earth export controls (Chinese dominance looms over chipmakers and defence contractors), AI governance, tariff relief, and the Taiwan arms question. Bloomberg notes that the summit is unlikely to produce structural breakthroughs — the most consequential dimensions of the rivalry (cyber, AI, export controls) remain deeply contested. Meanwhile chipmakers’ record Q3 sales are masking a worrying longer-term picture as the rare earth squeeze tightens, per The Economist.

🔥 World · Russia–Ukraine

Russia’s “Total War” Phase: Unprecedented Strain on Ukraine, Grey-Zone Attacks on Europe Grow Bolder

The Economist’s editors lead today’s newsletter with new on-the-ground reporting from Kyiv: Ukraine is feeling the pain of Russia’s shift to a phase of total war — physically, psychologically, economically and politically. Russia struck fuel tanks in Kyiv on September 22 while Ukraine destroyed a Russian ammunition depot with a drone strike. Separately, The Economist highlights that Russia’s grey-zone attacks on European countries supporting Ukraine — sabotage, drone incursions, airspace violations — are growing more brazen and are now actively shaping elections across the continent.

Zelenskyy expressed hope that the war could end before winter with Trump’s help and stated conditions for an energy ceasefire. Trump has signalled he could hold another meeting with Putin. NATO officials warn that the grey-zone campaign is probing for weaknesses in European air defences, and Germany is already planning to restrict the far-right AfD’s access to classified war planning intelligence.

❄️ World · Greenland / Arctic

Trump Drops Sovereignty Demands, Signs Greenland Military Deal — Broader US Arctic Footprint Without Annexation

President Trump signed an agreement with Greenland and Denmark on Tuesday that grants the United States a substantially expanded military presence in Greenland — broad overflight rights across the island and its territorial waters, plus a ban on non-NATO states establishing military installations there — but stops well short of his earlier demands for outright sovereignty transfer. The deal has no end date, and the Economist notes the harm to transatlantic trust from months of sovereignty threats may have already been done.

The agreement still requires approval from Danish and Greenlandic legislators, introducing political risk. The deal is partly driven by concern about Russian and Chinese activity in the Arctic shipping lanes opened by accelerating ice melt — a strategic waterway that will matter enormously within a decade.

🏠 Africa · Data Centres / Finance

Wall Street Turns Skeptical of the Data Centre Boom — IPOs Stall as Backlash Spreads to Investors

The NYT flags a significant shift in US capital markets: Wall Street is growing skeptical of the data centre buildout. SoftBank’s SB Energy — which had planned to IPO this month for the largest data centre project in the world in Ohio — has pushed back its listing to late October or beyond. Holtec and Aggreko have also delayed IPOs. Over 500 data centre bans and $68 billion in disrupted US projects are now threatening the projected $1 trillion AI infrastructure buildout by 2030. Companies tied to the sector had been expected to account for roughly one-third of all listings for the rest of 2026.

The backlash is rooted in community opposition to energy consumption, water use, and landscape impact. This does not kill the African opportunity — Lagos, Nairobi and Johannesburg face none of the nimby politics of Ohio or Virginia — but it does signal that the global capital that was expected to fund African data centre expansion may now cost more or flow more slowly. Nigeria races toward AI data centre ambitions with 146MW in the Lagos pipeline and a Central Bank mandate requiring local data storage, but energy reliability gaps remain the critical bottleneck.

🌿 Africa · Data Centres / Global Context

Australia’s “Lucky Country” Strikes AI Paydirt — $155 Billion Data Centre Pipeline Reshapes the Economy

The Economist’s lead story on business today is Australia’s staggering data centre boom: a $155 billion investment pipeline — equivalent to 5.6% of one year’s GDP — is being driven by AI demand, stable governance, and proximity to Asia-Pacific latency requirements. CommBank estimates data centres will add 0.2 percentage points to real GDP growth in both 2026 and 2027. Bloomberg Economics warns the pace risks overheating the economy, pushing inflation higher and forcing the Reserve Bank to keep rates elevated.

The contrast with Nigeria is instructive: Australia’s boom is constrained by electricity supply and skilled labour, while Nigeria’s constraint is unreliable grid power and security of physical infrastructure. The underlying AI demand driver, however, is identical — and Africa’s data-sovereignty and latency argument is as strong as Australia’s. The global pattern is now clear: any country that can offer stable power and sovereignty assurance will attract serious capital.

French Senate Election This Sunday (Sep 27) — Far Right Surge Tests Macron’s Rump Coalition

France’s Senate renews one-third of its seats on Sunday, September 27, in a vote that will test whether the right-wing and far-right wave that swept German state elections this month has spread to French upper-house politics. The Senate is already dominated by the centre-right Republicans, but polling suggests Marine Le Pen’s Rassemblement National is positioned to increase its Senate presence for the first time. The vote will affect the government’s ability to pass legislation and could reshape the dynamics ahead of the 2027 presidential cycle.

The broader European context is stark: the AfD won Mecklenburg-Vorpommern last week, hard-right parties are polling strongly in Austria and the Netherlands, and The Economist notes that Russia’s grey-zone operations are actively trying to radicalize European electorates in countries supporting Ukraine.

🏪 Real Estate · Beirut / Lebanon

Beirut Property Prices Now Only 10–15% Below Pre-2019 Levels — Supply Crunch Drives Paradoxical Recovery

Lebanon’s real estate market is in a paradoxical state: demand is strong and prices have risen 5–10% in 2026, closing to within 10–15% of pre-crisis (October 2019) valuations, yet the recovery is fragile. “Political stabilisation” following the recent government formation triggered a rush of transactions in Q1, with prices in Achrafieh now ranging from $300,000 for a compact two-bedroom to over $3 million for a full-floor luxury residence with sea views. New supply remains constrained by security uncertainty, the absence of bank finance and investor caution.

The recovery is geographically uneven: prime Beirut (Achrafieh, Hamra, Verdun) is outperforming peripheral areas still exposed to conflict risk. Bank Audi research highlights resilience in the upper market but warns that any renewed security deterioration could reverse gains quickly.

Update: Israel Links South Lebanon Withdrawal to Hezbollah Disarmament; Strikes Continue in Kfar Tibnit

Israel is publicly linking its long-overdue withdrawal from south Lebanon to the condition of Hezbollah disarmament and a stronger Lebanese Army — a position that effectively puts the ceasefire on indefinite hold. Israeli warplanes launched strikes on Mansouri and Kfar Tebnit in mid-September, wounding two Israeli soldiers when a device detonated inside Israel’s “security zone.” Israel accused Hezbollah of planting it. L’Orient Today (independent, Beirut) reports that Israeli Foreign Minister Katz has vowed a long-term Israeli presence in south Lebanon, while the US has publicly warned Israel against permanent occupation.

PM Salam used his UNGA meetings to stress continued US Army support for Lebanon and to push back diplomatically against the de facto Israeli occupation of buffer-zone villages. The Lebanese Army remains unable to deploy fully in several areas pending Israeli withdrawal, and 16,000 hectares in south Lebanon have been ravaged by fires since October 2023, complicating reconstruction.

🏭 Lebanon · Economy / IMF

IMF Mission Wraps Up Beirut Visit, Calls Deposit Law Amendments “Aligned” With Standards — But Goldman Sachs Flags Eurobonds as Overvalued

An IMF mission led by Ernesto Ramírez Rigo concluded its Beirut visit on September 18, calling recent amendments to the Bank Resolution Law “aligned with international standards” and reaffirming support for Lebanon’s reform agenda. The mission noted that economic activity is expected to contract significantly in 2026, inflation remains in double digits, and the current account deficit has widened. The remaining prerequisite for any IMF arrangement is passage of the Financial Stability and Deposit Recovery Law — which bank associations have resisted for years.

Separately, Goldman Sachs on September 4 said Lebanese Eurobonds look overvalued at current levels (BLOM Bond Index ~29.57), cautioning that passage of the Deposit Recovery Law is far from certain. The political economy is difficult: banks fear massive write-downs while depositors demand cash recovery. Any slip on the law will slam the bond market and push the IMF arrangement further back.
