⚓ World · Red Sea / Iran War (Update)

Update: Houthis Seize Perim Island — Iran-Aligned Forces Now Control Both Global Oil Chokepoints

A day after capturing Yemen’s Red Sea port of Mokha, Houthi forces moved swiftly to take Perim Island in the Bab el-Mandeb Strait — the narrow passage through which roughly 10% of global oil and vast quantities of consumer goods flow. Yemeni government forces withdrew without significant resistance. The seizure effectively gives Iran and its proxies domination over two critical maritime choke points simultaneously: the Bab el-Mandeb to the south-west of the Arabian Peninsula, and the Strait of Hormuz to its south-east — a strategic encirclement of the Gulf that no adversary has achieved before. Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman is privately urging President Trump to take military action against the Houthis; the White House has yet to respond publicly.

Oil eased slightly to ~$105/barrel on Friday as broader markets recovered, but the structural supply risk remains acute. Red Sea oil loadings have already fallen from 3.8 million barrels per day to 2.2 million bpd since August, according to the IEA. Shipping insurers are now pricing war-risk premiums on any vessel transiting within 150 nautical miles of the strait.

With both Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb now under threat, Lebanon’s import-dependent economy and Nigeria’s fuel-cost crisis get materially worse before they get better. 📈 World · Economy / Markets (Update)

Update: US Diesel Tops $6 for First Time Ever; 10-Year Treasury Near 5%; Fed Rate Hike Next Week 90% Priced

The global bond selloff deepened through the week as cascading pressures mounted: US core CPI in August rose 0.3% month-on-month, above the 0.2% consensus; average US diesel crossed $6 per gallon for the first time in history on Thursday morning; and Houthi attacks kept oil elevated. The 10-year US Treasury yield reached 4.94% — near the psychologically critical 5% level, its highest since 2023 — while the 30-year yield touched levels not seen since 2007. The Japanese 10-year moved above 3% for the first time since 1996. Markets priced a 90% probability of a 25bp Federal Reserve hike at the September 15–16 meeting, up from under 60% a week ago. The Economist’s financial editor explains the knock-on effects: pension funds are forced sellers, corporate debt rollovers become painful, and highly indebted sovereigns face refinancing at dramatically higher costs.

Despite the macro stress, US equity markets closed higher Friday: the Dow gained 509 points to 52,573, and the S&P 500 rose 0.9%, as CPI met headline expectations and traders positioned ahead of the Fed decision. President Trump is publicly pressuring Fed Chair Warsh not to hike — reigniting the central bank independence debate — and has threatened a trade embargo against any country if the Fed proceeds.

The Economist (anchor) · Bloomberg · CNBC · TheStreet
Why it matters to you

A Fed hike and sustained bond-yield elevation tightens dollar-borrowing costs globally, including for Lebanese clients trying to finance capital projects and Nigerian clients exposed to variable-rate debt. Higher US yields also push the Lebanese Eurobond further out of reach of restructuring on favourable terms, delaying the banking-sector recovery your Lebanese clients depend on.

🤝 World · Geopolitics (Update)

Update: BRICS Summit Concludes New Delhi — Modi Meets Xi, New Delhi Declaration Signed, Bloc’s Unity Tested

The 18th BRICS Leaders’ Summit concluded Saturday in New Delhi with the adoption of the New Delhi Declaration, committing the now-11-member bloc to cooperation on food and energy security, digital agriculture, AI-driven development systems, and climate resilience. India’s chairship manoeuvred to keep the joint communiqué coherent despite the extraordinary internal tension: Iran and the UAE — on opposite sides of the current Middle East conflict — are both full members. Modi held a high-profile bilateral with Chinese President Xi Jinping on Saturday, focusing on trade normalisation and border stabilisation; their meeting is their most substantive in three years. Modi-Putin talks on Friday addressed Ukraine, arms deals and energy.

The summit produced no joint statement on the Iran war — the bloc explicitly sidestepped it — but its geopolitical subtext was unmistakable: the New Delhi gathering represents the most powerful gathering of non-Western leaders since the Cold War. India is playing both sides with unusual dexterity, buying Russian oil, hosting both Iran and the UAE, and keeping its own borders with China stable enough to engage.

🏛️ World · Politics / 9/11 Legacy

Economist Cover: After 25 Years, the Post-9/11 Counterterrorism Arsenal Now Empowers Trump’s Presidency

The Economist’s cover story this week argues that the extraordinary executive powers Congress granted after September 11, 2001 — designed to fight Al-Qaeda — remain almost entirely on the books and have migrated to uses their authors never intended. President Trump has used counterterrorism-era authorities to classify drug smugglers as terrorist organisations, to justify domestic surveillance of political opponents, and to deploy emergency-powers frameworks against immigration and trade policy. The magazine draws a line from post-9/11 mission creep under Bush and Obama to the vastly more aggressive domestic use under Trump.

The editorial argues it may be time for Congress to revisit and sunset the 2001 and 2002 Authorisations for Use of Military Force (AUMFs) — something the legislative branch has so far proved unwilling to do despite bipartisan rhetoric. The Economist also notes that average US diesel surpassed $6 this week — a direct product of the Iran war that the expanded executive war-powers helped start — completing a circle from 9/11 to today’s energy shock.

🔢 World · AI / Science (Update)

Update: 24 Fields Medal Winners Denounce OpenAI’s Methods in Navier-Stokes “Proof” — Maths World in Uproar

Following OpenAI’s claim this week that its AI solved the Navier-Stokes Millennium Prize Problem in 88 hours, twenty-four holders of the Fields Medal — mathematics’ highest honour — co-signed an open letter attacking OpenAI’s methods and its approach to claiming mathematical credit. The signatories argue that OpenAI used the staggering resources of a trillion-dollar company to race ahead of individual researchers who were close to the same result, misrepresented the AI’s role, and bypassed the normal peer-review process. An NYU mathematician had been on a parallel path toward the same proof when OpenAI beat him to publication.

The controversy goes beyond credit: it raises profound questions about who — or what — “does” mathematics, who owns a proof, and whether AI systems can claim intellectual achievements. The NYT notes separately that OpenAI is facing a $1 million prize dispute with Tristan Buckmaster. The debate is likely to shape how mathematicians and scientists relate to AI for years.

📉 France · Economy (Update)

Update: France’s Economy Minister Officially Cuts Growth to 0.5%, Admits Deficit Will Exceed 5% of GDP

Economy Minister Roland Lescure announced Friday that the French government’s official 2026 growth forecast has been revised to 0.5% — a step below even Thursday’s INSEE figure of 0.4% — and explicitly acknowledged that the public deficit will overshoot its 5%-of-GDP budget target. The government’s own admission is significant: France is now the only large advanced economy expected to slow in 2026, and the deficit breach will put France back under European Commission fiscal-surveillance pressure. The convergence of oil shock, September heatwaves disrupting outdoor sectors, and a weakened consumer is proving more damaging than the government had assumed as recently as June.

The political backdrop remains precarious: Macron’s coalition controls neither the Assemblée Nationale nor the Sénat, making budget passage a high-wire act. Public transport disruptions continue across Île-de-France this weekend, with the RER D and RER B both suspended for planned maintenance works. Paris Design Week runs through September 19.

🚢 Lebanon · Trade / Economy

Lebanon’s Sea Import Lifeline Under New Threat as Houthis Seal Bab el-Mandeb — Cape Route Adds 15–20% to Cost

Lebanon is one of the most import-dependent economies in the world — food, fuel, medicine, machinery and building materials all arrive overwhelmingly by sea. A significant share of those goods, particularly electronics, machinery and consumer goods from Asia, transit through the Suez Canal and pass through the Bab el-Mandeb Strait. With Houthi forces now in control of both Mokha port and Perim Island, effective closure or severe risk-pricing of that corridor forces shipping to reroute around the Cape of Good Hope, adding 10–14 days of transit and 15–20% to freight costs on top of war-risk insurance premiums that are already at five-year highs. Lebanon’s already-strained import bill, which its central bank can barely finance in fresh dollars, faces another structural blow.

Separately, Lebanon’s parliament faces mounting pressure to pass the Deposit Recovery Law before the IMF’s October technical meeting — without it, the bank resolution law that the IMF called a “major step” in August cannot unlock a formal programme. BLOMINVEST estimates the BBI index at ~29.57, with Goldman Sachs still warning Lebanese Eurobonds are overvalued at current prices given legislative uncertainty.

Economist: War Has Not Halted the Gulf’s Data Centre Boom — Brookfield’s $20B Qatar Deal Confirmed Despite Drone Strikes

Despite AWS facilities in the UAE and Bahrain being struck by Iranian drones in March — causing outages across banking and enterprise services — the Gulf’s broader data centre ambitions remain firmly on track, according to The Economist. One week after the attacks, Brookfield Asset Management confirmed its $20 billion AI infrastructure partnership with Qatar Investment Authority would proceed as planned. The UAE government declared “no change” to its investment plans, and hyperscaler commitments to new Gulf campuses are being honoured. The logic: the region offers cheap energy, strategic geography between Europe and Asia, and sovereign AI ambitions backed by sovereign wealth. Investors see war-risk as manageable and temporary; demand is seen as permanent.

The global data centre real estate market is valued at $87.17 billion in 2026, growing at 13.4% annually, with Middle East investment expected to reach $33.79 billion cumulatively through 2030. Stabilised net operating income yields on data centre real estate exceed 10% — far ahead of office or retail — making it the most sought-after commercial property sector globally. The war disruption is creating a short-term opportunity for West Africa (particularly Lagos) to position as a lower-risk alternative hub.
